## 🧭 Web‑1.0 news design 
Early Web‑1.0 news design was an aesthetic choice — it was a *driven architecture*. It is the product of technological environment: connections, small screens, standards, and a design culture focused on reliability over over-expressiveness. The look is the visible surface of clarity concepts.

---

## 🧩 Core 

Early Web‑1.0 design was a **constraint‑optimized information delivery system** built for:

- low bandwidth  
- small displays  
- standards  
- content-based workflows  

Its defining characteristics — fixed-width tables, tiny images, dense hyperlinks, sidebar navigation — were stylistic choices in response to **engineering** of the era. Modern news sites operate under opposite constraints (monetization pressures), producing an entirely different visual and behavioral paradigm. The nostalgia comes from encountering a system designed for **clarity and load performance**, not metrics nor behavioral optimization. No cookie banners, no GDPR, no analytics scripts.  

The absence of:

- consent modals  
- tracking layers  
- personalization engines  

creates the “*refreshingly straightforward*” feel.

### **1. Constraint‑Driven Layout**
The entire design philosophy was shaped by hard limits:

- **Bandwidth:** 56k dial‑up meant pages had to be tiny.  
- **Screen size:** 800×600 forced fixed-width layouts.  

This produced the table‑heavy, pixel‑exact layouts that defined the era.

---

### **2. Table‑Based Structural Architecture**

Before CSS matured, HTML tables were the only reliable way to create multi-column layouts. 

Tables provided:
- predictable alignment  
- fixed cell widths  
- stable rendering across browsers  

This is why early news sites look “boxy”: the boxes *are literally table cells*.

---

### **3. Media Strategy**

Images were:

- small  
- heavily compressed  
- used sparingly  

This was stylistic minimalism — it was survival. A single 100KB image could stall a page for 10–20 seconds on dial‑up.

---

### **4. Hyperlink-Dominant Information Architecture**
Blue underlined links were the default browser style, and designers leaned into it.  
This created:
- long lists of links  
- text-first navigation  
- minimal visual hierarchy  

It’s the opposite of modern UX, which uses typography, spacing, and interaction patterns to guide attention.

---

### **5. Content Model**

Pages were:
- static files  
- non-interactive  
- free of client-side scripting (except gimmicks)

This meant:

- no autoplay video  
- no infinite scroll  
- no dynamic ad injection  

The site simply experienced as *served documents*.

---

### **6. Sidebar-Based Navigation**

The left sidebar became a standard pattern:

- tables made it easy  
- fixed-width layouts required predictable anchors  
- users expected persistent navigation

Dark was common because monitors rendered dark consistently.
