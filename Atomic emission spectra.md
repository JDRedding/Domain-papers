# Atomic emission spectra
**Key equations and formulas for flame test colors**

Flame colors arise because heat excites electrons in metal atoms/ions to higher discrete energy levels. When the electrons return to lower levels, they emit photons whose energy (and thus wavelength/color) equals the exact energy difference between those levels. These equations fully describe why different metals produce different flame colors: each element has a unique set of energy-level spacings, so each emits photons of characteristic wavelengths.

### 1. Photon energy

$$
E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

**Notation**  
- $E$: energy of the photon (joules or electronvolts)  
- $h$: Planck’s constant ($6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s)  
- $\nu$: frequency of the light (Hz)  
- $c$: speed of light in vacuum ($2.998 \times 10^{8}$ m/s)  
- $\lambda$: wavelength of the emitted light (m or nm)

The observed color is determined by $\lambda$ (visible range roughly 400–700 nm).

### 2. Energy difference between levels

$$
\Delta E = E_{\text{higher}} - E_{\text{lower}} = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

**Notation**  
- $\Delta E$: energy released in the transition  
- $E_{\text{higher}}$, $E_{\text{lower}}$: energies of the upper and lower electronic states

Only photons matching a specific $\Delta E$ for that element are emitted, producing the characteristic color (e.g., yellow for sodium, crimson for lithium).

### 3. Bohr model energies (exact for hydrogen; approximate starting point for alkali metals)

$$
E_n = -\frac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2} \quad (n = 1,2,3,\dots)
$$

or in joules:

$$
E_n = -\frac{k}{n^2}, \quad k = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\,\text{J}
$$

**Notation**  
- $n$: principal quantum number  
- The negative sign indicates bound states (zero energy at infinity).

### 4. Wavelength of emitted light (Rydberg formula, hydrogen)

$$
\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_\infty\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2}-\frac{1}{n_2^2}\right) \quad (n_2 > n_1)
$$

**Notation**  
- $R_\infty$: Rydberg constant ($1.097 \times 10^{7}$ m$^{-1}$)  
- $n_1$: lower level  
- $n_2$: upper level  

For multi-electron atoms (Na, Li, K, Ca, etc. used in flame tests) the exact energies are more complex and require quantum mechanics (Hartree–Fock or experimental term values), but the same $\Delta E = hc/\lambda$ relation still governs the observed color.

### 5. Relationship between energy and color (practical form)

$$
\lambda\,(\text{nm}) = \frac{1240}{E\,(\text{eV})}
$$

This convenient conversion lets you go directly from a measured energy gap to the wavelength (and therefore the flame color).
