# SimEarth
**SimEarth (1990)** still stands out as one of the most ambitious planet-scale simulations ever attempted, and the post you’re reacting to taps directly into why it left such a strange, lasting imprint. The short version: **SimEarth wasn’t really a game so much as a planetary-evolution laboratory disguised as entertainment** — which is exactly why that interface still hits people. 

### What made SimEarth

SimEarth was Maxis’s second major simulation after *SimCity*, designed by Will Wright and heavily influenced by James Lovelock’s **Gaia hypothesis**, which treats Earth as a self-regulating system. Lovelock consulted on the project, contributed an introductory essay, and saw his Daisyworld model turned into an in-game tutorial.

A few defining traits:

- **Planetary management instead of city management.** You controlled atmosphere composition, continental drift, volcanic activity, mutation rates, and climate feedback loops.
- **A 10-billion-year simulation arc.** The planet ages until the Sun becomes a red giant.
- **Emergent life.** Microbes could give way to animals, intelligent species, nuclear wars, and even machine civilizations.
- **Scenarios** ranging from Cambrian Earth to terraforming Mars or Venus to rescuing a future Earth from self-replicating robots.
- **Daisyworld.** A simplified climate-regulation model used as a tutorial, drawn directly from Lovelock’s research.

It was less immediately “fun” than *SimCity*. Critics praised the ambition while noting that the game was often admired more than actually played because of its density and opaque interface. The geosphere / hydrosphere / atmosphere sliders, the pixelated world map, the “New Planet” prompt — instantly evokes an era when simulation games were dense, scientific, and unapologetically weird.

For many players, SimEarth was a first encounter with:

- planetary science
- climate feedback loops
- evolutionary biology
- terraforming
- systems thinking

Some writers have argued that SimEarth helped popularize Earth-system science for a generation of players. So when someone posts that interface today, it isn’t just nostalgia for a game. It’s nostalgia for a moment when PC sims felt like portals into scientific ideas.
