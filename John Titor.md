# John Titor

**John Titor** is a mysterious online personality who claimed to be a time traveler from 2036. His posts sparked decades of fascination, detailed predictions, and ongoing debate about whether the story was an elaborate hoax, performance art, or something else. John Titor remains part of internet folklore. Most of his specific predictions — the *2004 civil war*, the *2015 nuclear war* — did ***not*** happen. What lasted was the mix of technical-sounding detail, philosophical talk about time travel, and a story that invited readers to argue, investigate, and keep adding to the myth.

The saga works as both a cautionary tale about taking anonymous online claims at face value and an early example of internet storytelling that blended science fiction, urban legend, and participatory discussion.

In the end, John Titor sits at the intersection of internet hoaxes, early online culture, and the lasting human fascination with time travel. His identity, and the nature of the claims, remain unresolved.

## Origins and Forum Activity

John Titor first appeared in 1998 through faxes sent to radio host Art Bell on *Coast to Coast AM*, claiming that time travel would be invented in 2034 through research at CERN.

The persona began posting more fully under the username **TimeTravel_0** on the Time Travel Institute forums in November 2000. He said he was a U.S. military soldier from 2036, sent back to retrieve an IBM 5100 computer from 1975. According to him, the machine’s hidden capabilities would help solve future computing problems.

By January 2001 he was using the name John Titor and also posting on Art Bell’s Post-2-Post forums. His last known post appeared in March 2001. After that, he vanished from the internet.

## Claims and Time Machine

Titor described a “*stationary mass, temporal displacement unit*” powered by dual top-spin singularities—essentially miniature black holes—capable of bending spacetime.

He said he installed the device in a 1967 Chevrolet Corvette for travel to 1975, then later transferred it to a 1987 four-wheel-drive vehicle during his stopover in 2000. His posts included technical diagrams, operational details, and photographs, which added to the story’s apparent realism.

## Predictions and Worldview

Titor’s predictions included a U.S. civil war beginning around 2004, a nation eventually split into five regions, and a brief nuclear World War III in 2015. He also spoke of societal collapse, Y2K-related crises, and outbreaks such as Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease.

He frequently invoked the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. In that framing, interacting with the past created divergent timelines—an explanation that later served as a ready-made reason why many of his predictions did not occur in “our” worldline.

One technical detail that later attracted attention was his claim that the IBM 5100 had a hidden ability to debug legacy systems. Programmers familiar with the machine confirmed that such a capability existed, which gave some of his early technical claims an extra layer of credibility.

## Investigations and Speculations

No one has confirmed Titor’s real identity. Investigations have pointed toward Larry Haber, a Florida lawyer, and possibly his brothers as potential creators of the persona. They have consistently denied authorship.

A company associated with the story, the John Titor Foundation, published compilations of the posts. The narrative later inspired literary and pop-culture works, most notably the Japanese visual novel *Steins;Gate*, which uses ideas such as divergent worldlines and a mission to retrieve a vintage computer.
