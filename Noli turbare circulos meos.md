# Noli turbare circulos meos

Archimedes almost certainly **did not** say *“Noli turbare circulos meos!”* — but he **did** say something like *“Do not disturb that diagram,”* according to Valerius Maximus. The specific “circles” wording is a *later medieval embellishment*, not an ancient quote.

## **What the document actually says (with citations)**

### **1. The ancient core of the story**
Earliest surviving account is from **Valerius Maximus** (early 1st century AD), who records Archimedes pleading:

> *“noli obsecro istum disturbare”*  
> (“do not, I beg you, disturb that”) 

This is the **only** ancient Latin plea we have.  
It does **not** mention circles.

Livy and Plutarch both describe Archimedes dying while absorbed in a diagram, but **neither gives a quote**. Plutarch even gives **three different versions** of the death, none containing the “circles” line. 

### **2. Where “circles” actually comes from**

> “the specific reference to ‘circles’ likely originated as a later embellishment… earliest recorded version appearing in later traditions.” 

The first known appearance of the “circles” wording is in **John Tzetzes**, 12th century, who writes in Greek:

> *“Stand away, fellow, from my diagram.”*  
> (Μὴ μου τοὺς κύκλους τάραττε) 

Even here, “circles” is **not** guaranteed — Tzetzes’ Greek can mean “my diagram,” and the “circles” interpretation is a later Latinization.

### **3. Renaissance humanists popularized the Latin form**
Latin phrase **Noli turbare circulos meos** became widespread only after the **1470 Mentelin edition** of Valerius Maximus and later humanist commentary. 

Humanists like Erasmus helped canonize the dramatic version.

### **4. Modern meaning**
The phrase survives because it perfectly symbolizes:

- total immersion in mathematical thought  
- the tension between abstract reasoning and worldly chaos  
- the romantic image of the scholar who dies protecting a diagram

> “a cultural emblem of focused scholarship and resistance to interruption.” 

## **Archimedes’ real last words**
Based strictly on ancient evidence:

### **Most authentic ancient version**
**“Noli obsecro istum disturbare.”**  
(“Do not, I beg you, disturb that.”)

### **Medieval Greek version**
**“Μὴ μου τοὺς κύκλους τάραττε.”**  
(“Do not disturb my circles/diagram.”)

### **Renaissance Latin slogan**
**“Noli turbare circulos meos!”**

Only the **first** is ancient.  
The others are **later literary upgrades**.
