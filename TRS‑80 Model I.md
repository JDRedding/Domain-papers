# **TRS‑80 Model I**

The **TRS‑80 Model I (1977)** was one of the three machines that formed the legendary *1977 Trinity* of early personal computing, alongside the Apple II and Commodore PET. Its significance comes from being the **first mass‑market, fully assembled microcomputer sold through a nationwide retail chain**, making computing accessible to ordinary households.  

### 📜 Historical placement
The TRS‑80 Model I is one of the most important early microcomputers because it:
- democratized computing through retail availability  
- established the Z80 as a dominant 8‑bit CPU  
- created a massive hobbyist and software ecosystem  
- outsold the Apple II by **5×** until 1982 (per one analysis) 

---

### 🖥️ What the TRS‑80 Model I *was*
A **Zilog Z80‑based** home computer released by Tandy/Radio Shack on **August 3, 1977**, priced at **$599.95** for the full system (keyboard/computer, 12" monitor, cassette recorder).   

It shipped with:
- **4 KB RAM** (expandable to 48 KB)  
- **Level I BASIC** in ROM (4 KB)  
- **1.77 MHz Z80 CPU**  
- **Cassette storage** (no floppy at launch)  
- **Uppercase‑only display** on a **64×16 monochrome CRT**  
- **Semigraphics** using “squots” (48×128 resolution) 

---

### ⚙️ Quirks and limitations
- **Cassette I/O was slow and unreliable** (250–500 baud).  
- **Keyboard bounce issues** and **expansion interface instability** earned it the nickname **“Trash‑80.”** 
- **Massive RF interference** eventually caused the Model I to be **discontinued in 1981** under stricter FCC rules.

---

### 🧩 Expansion ecosystem
The Model I could grow into a full workstation:
- **Expansion Interface**: RS‑232, floppy controller, extra RAM  
- **Up to four floppy drives**  
- **Hard drive options (5 MB)**  
- **Voice synthesizer**, printers, modems, and third‑party upgrades 

---

### 🧰 Why it mattered
- **First widely available consumer microcomputer** — sold in thousands of Radio Shack stores.  
- **Exploded demand**: Radio Shack expected to sell ~600 units; instead sold **10,000 in the first month** and **100,000 in 1978**.  
- **Largest software ecosystem** in the microcomputer market by 1979. 
- **Catalyzed home computing**: finance tools, recipe storage, games, education software, and hobbyist programming.
