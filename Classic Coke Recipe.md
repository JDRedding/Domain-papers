# Coca-Cola Flavor Architecture 
Classic Reconstruction

LabCoatz (Zach Armstrong) spent about a year matching commercial Coke with GC-MS help from other chemistry channels, published literature, and iterative tasting. The result is a two-phase home formula: an ethanol “7X-like” oil concentrate and a water-phase acid/color/tannin matrix. He later updated the batch from the original video amounts to 10 g tannin, 150 g glycerin, 43 mL of 85% phosphoric acid, and 185 mL Durkee caramel in the 1 L water phase. Your writeup follows that later cut.

- **Functional analog.** The major known Coca-Cola levers are present: sucrose or invert sugar, phosphoric acid, caffeine near 96 mg/L, caramel color, citrus-spice oils, vanilla, a little acetic acid, and an astringent dry-down.
- **Not vault formula.** Coca-Cola treats Merchandise 7X and the remaining “natural flavors” as a trade secret. A close mass spectrum of *volatiles* plus informal blind tasting does largely prove identity of nonvolatiles, enantiomer ratios, emulsion system, aging protocol, or the actual botanical sources.

The core is that Coca-Cola’s “secret flavor” is not one exotic ingredient. It is a **precise geometric blend of citrus terpenes, spice volatiles, caramelization products, acids, and tannins**, tuned through **acid-catalyzed hydrolysis**. Modern GC-MS work is used to support that structure. Coca-Cola’s taste can be described as a **citrus–spice terpene lattice** — mainly limonene, α-terpineol, fenchol, cinnamaldehyde, and sabinene — supported by:

- phosphoric acid
- caramelization compounds
- caffeine
- wine tannins

The tannins are used to recreate the dryness once associated with coca-leaf extract. In the reported blind tests, a reconstructed 7X concentrate plus syrup was described as **indistinguishable** from commercial Coke.

## Reconstructed formula 
The interesting move in the reconstruction is not a hidden exotic molecule. It is that **GC-MS cannot see the mouthfeel problem**. Tannins do not vaporize in a standard gas-phase run, so a replica can look “almost identical” on a chromatogram and still drink like a thin, too-sweet cola until an astringent fraction is added. That is a real analytical limitation, not marketing.

### Scientific reconstruction

GC-MS and hydrolysis experiments are used to argue that:

- **α-terpineol and fenchol** supply leafy-fresh notes; α-terpineol can form from acid-driven breakdown of citrus terpenes
- **Limonene** supports lemon/lime oil as the citrus backbone
- **Acid-catalyzed hydrolysis** during syrup heating partially inverts sucrose and softens harsh citrus terpene notes
- **Wine tannins** are essential to mouthfeel and dryness

## Core flavor architecture

### Citrus terpenes
Limonene, α-terpineol, α-terpinene, fenchol.

These supply the top-note sparkle and the recognizable Coke brightness. GC-MS is described as showing them as dominant peaks.

### Spice volatiles

- Cinnamaldehyde from cassia cinnamon
- Sabinene and carene from nutmeg
- Coriander-derived compounds

These add warmth, depth, and the “cola” identity.

### Caramelization products
Furfural and ethyl levulinate.

These contribute color, burnt-sugar sweetness, and base notes.

### Acid system
Phosphoric acid plus a trace of acetic acid.

This supplies sharpness, balance, and the characteristic bite.

### Tannins
Wine tannins are used to approximate the dryness once produced by coca-leaf extract. That substitution is presented as a major reconstruction insight.

### Alcohol-based concentrate
An essential-oil blend forming the aromatic core:

A chemically grounded flavor architecture, not a manufacturing SOP. Coca-Cola’s profile can be described as two separately prepared systems:

- **Mixture A** — a terpene–spice concentrate (alcohol phase)
- **Mixture B** — a water-phase acid–tannin–caramel matrix

Those two systems are then dosed in very small amounts into a sugar solution and carbonated water.

## Mixture A — Flavor Concentrate
The described preparation diluted **20 mL of the oil blend** into **1 L of 95% ethanol**. Alcohol-based aromatic core.

### Oil blend composition
The blend is dominated by **limonene**, **α-terpineol**, **fenchol**, **cinnamaldehyde**, and **sabinene**. Those volatiles were described as the GC-MS backbone of the Coca-Cola aroma profile.

## Mixture B — Water-Phase Matrix
This phase supplies **acidity**, **dryness**, **color**, **body**, and the characteristic cola “bite.”

- Glycerin
- Phosphoric acid
- Caffeine
- Sugar, heated to induce partial inversion
- Wine tannins

## Final Assembly
The two-system model is the correct way to think about cola:

- **Mixture A** is the odor vector: terpenes and phenylpropanoids dissolved in ethanol so they disperse at parts-per-million in the finished drink.
- **Mixture B** is the somatosensory matrix: sourness, bitterness, color, viscosity, and dryness.

That split matches how flavor houses actually work. Oils live in alcohol or an emulsion. Acids, caffeine, color, and polyphenols live in water.

Ratio for **1 L of finished cola**:

- 104 g sugar, dissolved in minimal water
- 1 mL Mixture A
- 10 mL Mixture B
- Cold carbonated water to volume

Combine with syrup, dilute with carbonated water, adjust sweetness/acidity, and chill. Diluted with water to a total volume of **1 L**.

| Component | Amount |
|---|---|
| Durkee caramel color | 185 mL |
| Vegetable glycerin | 150 g |
| 85% phosphoric acid | 43 mL |
| Wine tannin | 10 g |
| Caffeine | 9.65 g |
| 5% vinegar | 10 mL |
| Vanilla extract | 10 mL |

The oil blend totals exactly 100.0 mL. Diluting 20 mL of that blend to 1 L ethanol, then dosing 1 mL into 1 L soda, puts **20 µL of total essential oil per liter** in the glass. That is a normal beverage flavoring load, not a perfume dose.

Finished cola as:

$$
\text{Coke analog} = \text{sugar water} + \text{CO}_2 + \varepsilon_A(\text{oils}) + \varepsilon_B(\text{acid + tannin + color + caffeine})
$$

with $\varepsilon_A \approx 0.1\%$ of concentrate A and $\varepsilon_B = 1\%$ of concentrate B.

| Finished drink | This reconstruction | Commercial Coke ballpark |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar | 104 g/L | about 106–110 g/L depending on market |
| Caffeine | 96.5 mg/L | about 96–97 mg/L |
| Phosphoric acid | roughly 0.62 g/L as H₃PO₄ | about 0.54 g/L in one Coca-Cola Enterprises spec |
| Acetic acid from 5% vinegar | about 5 mg/L | 3.4 mg/L in the 2014 Coca-Cola quantitation |
| Wine tannin | 100 mg/L | not a labeled Coke ingredient; used here as a coca-leaf/tea dryness proxy |

Caffeine is the cleanest hit. Sugar is a little low for U.S. Coke and close for some international sucrose versions. Phosphoric acid is a bit high, which would sharpen bite and can read as “more Coke than Pepsi” if everything else is balanced.

## Blind taste-test claim

The ratio reported as indistinguishable from commercial Coca-Cola in blind tasting.

Across the reported trials, participants could not distinguish LabCoatz’s Lab Cola from commercial Coca-Cola; some preferred the replica.

## Finial notes

- 85% phosphoric acid is corrosive. Glass only.
- Essential oils are concentrated irritants until diluted.
- Caffeine at 9.65 g/L in concentrate B is a real dose in the stock solution; weigh it, do not approximate.
- Caramel color strength varies by brand, which is why 320 mL Shank’s became 185 mL Durkee.
- Heating drives useful chemistry and also drives off top notes if the vessel is open.

## 1. Sets and composition vectors

Let the finished beverage volume be $V$ (here $V = 1\,\mathrm{L}$).

**Oil‑blend components**

$$
\mathcal{O} = \{\text{lemon}, \text{lime}, \text{tea tree}, \text{cassia}, \text{nutmeg}, \text{orange}, \text{coriander}, \text{fenchol}\}.
$$

**Water‑phase stock components**

$$
\mathcal{W} = \{\text{caramel}, \text{glycerin}, \text{H}_3\mathrm{PO}_4, \text{tannin}, \text{caffeine}, \text{acetic acid}, \text{vanilla}\}.
$$

**Volatile odorants tracked after processing**

$$
\mathcal{A} = \{\text{limonene}, \alpha\text{-terpineol}, \text{4-terpineol}, \text{fenchol}, \text{cinnamaldehyde}, \text{sabinene}, \ldots\}.
$$

**Stock volumes in the oil blend**

$$
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{oil}} = (v_i)_{i\in\mathcal{O}}, \qquad V_{\mathrm{oil}} = \sum_{i\in\mathcal{O}} v_i = 100.0\,\mathrm{mL}.
$$

**Volume fractions in the oil blend**

$$
\phi_i = \frac{v_i}{V_{\mathrm{oil}}}, \qquad \sum_{i\in\mathcal{O}} \phi_i = 1.
$$

Explicitly,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi_{\mathrm{lemon}} &= 0.458, & \phi_{\mathrm{lime}} &= 0.365, & \phi_{\mathrm{TTO}} &= 0.080, \\
\phi_{\mathrm{cassia}} &= 0.045, & \phi_{\mathrm{nutmeg}} &= 0.027, & \phi_{\mathrm{orange}} &= 0.012, \\
\phi_{\mathrm{coriander}} &= 0.007, & \phi_{\mathrm{fenchol}} &= 0.006.
\end{aligned}
$$

## 3. Final assembly

For one litre of finished cola the reconstruction uses

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{sucrose}} &= 104\,\mathrm{g}, \\
V_A^{\mathrm{dose}} &= 1\,\mathrm{mL} = 1.0\times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{L}, \\
V_B^{\mathrm{dose}} &= 10\,\mathrm{mL} = 1.0\times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{L}, \\
V_{\mathrm{syrup}} + V_{\mathrm{carb}} &= V = 1\,\mathrm{L}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Dose factors from concentrates into the finished drink**

$$
\varepsilon_A = \frac{V_A^{\mathrm{dose}}}{V} = 1.0\times 10^{-3}, \qquad
\varepsilon_B = \frac{V_B^{\mathrm{dose}}}{V} = 1.0\times 10^{-2}.
$$

**Finished‑drink operator**

$$
\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{fin}} = \varepsilon_A\,\mathbf{c}^{A} + \varepsilon_B\,\mathbf{c}^{B} + \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{sugar}} + \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{CO}_2}.
$$

Componentwise:

$$
c^{\mathrm{fin}}_i = \varepsilon_A\,\delta_A\,\phi_i\,\rho_i = 2.0\times 10^{-5}\,\phi_i\,\rho_i \qquad (i\in\mathcal{O}),
$$

$$
c^{\mathrm{fin}}_j = \varepsilon_B\,c^{B}_j = 10^{-2}\,c^{B}_j \qquad (j\in\mathcal{W}).
$$

**Total neat oil in the finished drink**

$$
V_{\mathrm{oil}}^{\mathrm{fin}} = \varepsilon_A\,\delta_A\,V = 20\,\mu\mathrm{L\,L}^{-1}.
$$

**Oil $i$ in the finished drink**

$$
V_{i}^{\mathrm{fin}} = \phi_i\,V_{\mathrm{oil}}^{\mathrm{fin}}.
$$

So

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\mathrm{lemon}}^{\mathrm{fin}} &= 9.16\,\mu\mathrm{L\,L}^{-1}, \\
V_{\mathrm{lime}}^{\mathrm{fin}} &= 7.30\,\mu\mathrm{L\,L}^{-1}, \\
V_{\mathrm{TTO}}^{\mathrm{fin}} &= 1.60\,\mu\mathrm{L\,L}^{-1}, \\
V_{\mathrm{cassia}}^{\mathrm{fin}} &= 0.90\,\mu\mathrm{L\,L}^{-1}, \\
V_{\mathrm{nutmeg}}^{\mathrm{fin}} &= 0.54\,\mu\mathrm{L\,L}^{-1}, \\
V_{\mathrm{orange}}^{\mathrm{fin}} &= 0.24\,\mu\mathrm{L\,L}^{-1}, \\
V_{\mathrm{coriander}}^{\mathrm{fin}} &= 0.14\,\mu\mathrm{L\,L}^{-1}, \\
V_{\mathrm{fenchol}}^{\mathrm{fin}} &= 0.12\,\mu\mathrm{L\,L}^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

## 5. Acid system and bite

Phosphoric acid is triprotic:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{H_3PO_4} &\rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{H_2PO_4^-}, & K_{a1} &= 7.1\times 10^{-3}, \\
\mathrm{H_2PO_4^-} &\rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{HPO_4^{2-}}, & K_{a2} &= 6.3\times 10^{-8}, \\
\mathrm{HPO_4^{2-}} &\rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{PO_4^{3-}}, & K_{a3} &= 4.2\times 10^{-13}.
\end{aligned}
$$

At cola pH only the first equilibrium matters. Charge‑balance approximation for a strong‑enough first proton:

$$
[\mathrm{H^+}]^2 - K_{a1}C \approx 0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\mathrm{pH} \approx -\log_{10}\sqrt{K_{a1}[\mathrm{H_3PO_4}]}.
$$

Carbonation adds a second weak acid:

$$
\mathrm{CO_2(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2O} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_2CO_3} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{HCO_3^-}.
$$

If carbonation is reported in volumes $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{CO_2}}$ (commercial Coke $\approx 3.7$),

$$
[\mathrm{CO_2}] \approx \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\,\frac{P^\circ}{RT}\,s(T),
$$

where $s(T)$ is Henry’s constant in the chosen units.

A scalar “bite” coordinate used later:

$$
B = w_{\mathrm{p}}\,[\mathrm{H^+}]_{\mathrm{phos}} + w_{\mathrm{c}}\,[\mathrm{H^+}]_{\mathrm{CO_2}} + w_{\mathrm{a}}\,c^{\mathrm{fin}}_{\mathrm{HOAc}}.
$$

## 6. Sucrose inversion during syrup heat

Reaction:

$$
\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H^+}} \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}.
$$

Let $S(t)$ be sucrose molarity in the hot syrup. Under excess water and constant acidity,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t} = -k_{\mathrm{inv}}(T,a_{\mathrm{H^+}})\,S,
$$

$$
S(t) = S_0\,e^{-k_{\mathrm{inv}} t}, \qquad X_{\mathrm{inv}}(t) = 1-e^{-k_{\mathrm{inv}} t}.
$$

Arrhenius form:

$$
k_{\mathrm{inv}}(T,a_{\mathrm{H^+}}) = A\,a_{\mathrm{H^+}}\,e^{-E_a/RT}.
$$

Invert‑sugar mass after heating:

$$
m_{\mathrm{inv}}(t) = m_{\mathrm{sucrose}}\,X_{\mathrm{inv}}(t)\cdot\frac{342.3+18.0}{342.3}
\approx m_{\mathrm{sucrose}}\,X_{\mathrm{inv}}(t).
$$

Remaining sucrose / formed hexoses:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{\mathrm{suc}}(t) &= c^{\mathrm{fin}}_{\mathrm{suc}}\bigl(1-X_{\mathrm{inv}}(t)\bigr), \\
c_{\mathrm{glc}}(t) = c_{\mathrm{frc}}(t) &= \tfrac12\,c^{\mathrm{fin}}_{\mathrm{suc}}\,X_{\mathrm{inv}}(t)\cdot\frac{180.16}{171.15}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Sweetness is not linear in mass. A first‑order mixing rule:

$$
\Sigma(t) = \sigma_{\mathrm{suc}}c_{\mathrm{suc}}(t) + \sigma_{\mathrm{glc}}c_{\mathrm{glc}}(t) + \sigma_{\mathrm{frc}}c_{\mathrm{frc}}(t),
$$

with typical relative sweetness weights $\sigma_{\mathrm{suc}}=1$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{glc}}\approx 0.7$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{frc}}\approx 1.2$.

## 7. Acid‑catalyzed terpene map

The reconstruction’s key citrus rearrangement is limonene hydration to $\alpha$-terpineol. A lumped scheme:

$$
\mathrm{limonene} \xrightarrow{k_1(a_{\mathrm{H^+}},T)} \alpha\text{-terpineol}
\xrightarrow{k_2} \text{other oxides / cineole-like sinks}.
$$

Let $L(t)$ and $\tau(t)$ be the corresponding concentrations in the syrup:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}t} &= -k_1 L, \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}t} &= k_1 L - k_2 \tau.
\end{aligned}
$$

Solution from $L(0)=L_0$, $\tau(0)=\tau_0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(t) &= L_0 e^{-k_1 t}, \\
\tau(t) &= \tau_0 e^{-k_2 t} + \frac{k_1 L_0}{k_2-k_1}\bigl(e^{-k_1 t}-e^{-k_2 t}\bigr) \quad (k_1\neq k_2).
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the precise content of the claim that “acid‑catalyzed hydrolysis softens harsh citrus terpene notes”: $L$ falls while $\tau$ rises, so the top‑note vector rotates from hydrocarbon sparkle toward leafy‑oxygenated citrus.

A source term from tea‑tree oil can be added for 4‑terpineol,

$$
\tau_4^{\mathrm{fin}} = \varepsilon_A\delta_A\phi_{\mathrm{TTO}}\rho_{\mathrm{TTO}}\,w_{4\mathrm{-terp}} + \tau_4^{\mathrm{process}},
$$

where $w_{4\mathrm{-terp}}$ is the mass fraction of terpinen‑4‑ol in the oil. That term is chemically the reason TTO was introduced; it is not a food‑legal necessity.

## 8. Odor‑activity and the abundance/impact distinction

For odorant $\alpha\in\mathcal{A}$,

$$
\mathrm{OAV}_\alpha = \frac{C_\alpha}{\theta_\alpha},
$$

where $C_\alpha$ is the finished concentration and $\theta_\alpha$ is the odor‑detection threshold in the same units.

A linear odor‑impact vector

$$
\mathbf{o} = \bigl(\mathrm{OAV}_\alpha\bigr)_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
$$

is the correct “backbone,” not the raw GC‑MS peak vector $\mathbf{p}$. The two are related by a diagonal threshold matrix $\Theta = \mathrm{diag}(\theta_\alpha)$:

$$
\mathbf{o} = \Theta^{-1}\mathbf{C}, \qquad \mathbf{C} \propto \mathbf{p}\ \text{only after response-factor calibration}.
$$

A compact mismatch functional between “peak lattice” and “nose lattice”:

$$
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{MS}} = \left\| \frac{\mathbf{p}}{\|\mathbf{p}\|_1} - \frac{\mathbf{o}}{\|\mathbf{o}\|_1} \right\|_1.
$$

For cola this $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{MS}}$ is large: limonene and $\alpha$-terpineol dominate $\mathbf{p}$, while cineole, $(R)$-linalool and octanal dominate $\mathbf{o}$.

A group‑omission test, as in the 2014 reconstitution work, is

$$
\Delta S(\mathcal{G}) = \|\,\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{C}) - \mathbf{s}(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{G}})\|
$$

for a spice subset

$$
\mathcal{G}=\{\text{cinnamaldehyde}, \text{eugenol}, \text{isoeugenols}, \text{methyleugenol}\}.
$$

The published result is $\Delta S(\mathcal{G})>0$ while single‑component omissions are not significant. That is a set‑norm statement, not a one‑molecule statement.

## 9. Flavor geometry

Write the finished state as a pair of vectors

$$
\mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{top}}, \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{base}}),
$$

with

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{top}} &= \bigl(c_{\mathrm{lim}},\, c_{\tau},\, c_{\mathrm{fenchol}},\, c_{\mathrm{sabinene}},\, c_{\mathrm{cineole}},\, c_{\mathrm{linalool}},\, c_{\mathrm{alkanals}}\bigr), \\
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{base}} &= \bigl(c_{\mathrm{cin}},\, c_{\mathrm{van}},\, c_{\mathrm{caf}},\, c_{\mathrm{tan}},\, c_{\mathrm{H_3PO_4}},\, c_{\mathrm{glyc}},\, c_{\mathrm{caramel}}\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$

**Scale operators** (positive diagonal matrices of perceptual gains):

$$
T = \mathrm{diag}(t_\alpha), \qquad K = \mathrm{diag}(k_\beta).
$$

**Top‑note energy and base‑note energy**

$$
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}) = \|T\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{top}}\|_2^2, \qquad
\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}) = \|K\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{base}}\|_2^2.
$$

The “scale‑tension” scalar is the imbalance

$$
\Psi(\mathbf{x}) = \log\frac{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon}{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon}.
$$

- $\Psi>0$: too bright / solvent‑citrus / “Sprite‑like”
- $\Psi<0$: too heavy / burnt / astringent
- $\Psi\approx 0$: the intended cola balance

A reconstruction target is then

$$
\min_{\mathbf{x}} \quad \lambda_1\|\mathbf{o}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{o}^\star\|_2^2 + \lambda_2\bigl(\Psi(\mathbf{x})-\Psi^\star\bigr)^2 + \lambda_3\bigl(B(\mathbf{x})-B^\star\bigr)^2 + \lambda_4\bigl(\Sigma(\mathbf{x})-\Sigma^\star\bigr)^2,
$$

where $\mathbf{o}^\star, \Psi^\star, B^\star, \Sigma^\star$ are Coca‑Cola references.

## 10. A relational‑style operator on the flavor manifold

The writeup’s “RDG operator” is not a standard beverage‑science object. The usable analogue is a reduced directional gradient on the composition manifold.

Let $S(\mathbf{x})$ be a scalar sensory score against commercial Coke. The ordinary gradient

$$
\nabla S = \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial x_k}\right)
$$

is noisy because ingredients are not independent (oils covary, heating couples $L$ and $\tau$, tannin changes apparent sweetness). A reduced operator keeps only the empirically stiff directions

$$
\mathcal{R} = \{\text{citrus terpenes},\ \text{spice phenylpropanoids},\ \text{acid},\ \text{tannin},\ \text{sugar},\ \text{carbonation}\}.
$$

Define the projection $P_{\mathcal{R}}$ onto those coordinates and

$$
\mathrm{RDG}[S](\mathbf{x}) = \frac{P_{\mathcal{R}}\nabla S(\mathbf{x})}{\|P_{\mathcal{R}}\nabla S(\mathbf{x})\|_2+\epsilon}.
$$

A line‑search update for a new batch is

$$
\mathbf{x}^{(n+1)}
= \mathbf{x}^{(n)} + \eta_n\,\mathrm{RDG}[S](\mathbf{x}^{(n)}).
$$

If one wants an explicit geometric product rather than a gradient name, the citrus–spice lattice can be written as a rank‑1 coupling

$$

## 12. End‑to‑end generation map

Collecting the process into one composition of maps:

$$
\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{fin}} = \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{carb}} \circ \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{heat}} \circ \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dose}} \bigl(\mathbf{c}^{A},\mathbf{c}^{B},m_{\mathrm{sucrose}}\bigr),
$$

where

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dose}}(\mathbf{c}^{A},\mathbf{c}^{B},m_{\mathrm{suc}}) &= \varepsilon_A\mathbf{c}^{A}+\varepsilon_B\mathbf{c}^{B}+\tfrac{m_{\mathrm{suc}}}{V}\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{suc}}, \\
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{heat}} &= \text{inversion }X_{\mathrm{inv}}(t,T,a_{\mathrm{H^+}}) \text{ plus terpene map }(L,\tau), \\
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{carb}} &= \text{addition of }[\mathrm{CO_2}]\text{ and dilution to }V.
\end{aligned}
$$

Numerical values of the dose maps for this specific formula:

$$
\varepsilon_A\delta_A = 2.0\times 10^{-5}, \qquad \varepsilon_B = 1.0\times 10^{-2}.
$$

Those two numbers, together with $\phi$, $\mathbf{c}^{B}$, $X_{\mathrm{inv}}$, and $D$, are the entire reconstruction in equation form.
