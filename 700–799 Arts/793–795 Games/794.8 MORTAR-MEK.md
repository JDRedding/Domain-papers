# MORTAR-MEK
**794.8 — Game design**

While Mortar focuses on *automatic* evolution of mechanics expressed as code, there exists a parallel line of research aimed at making mechanics easier for *human designers* to express, test, and iterate. One representative system is **Mek**, a domain‑specific language and prototyping tool for **2D, turn‑based, tile‑based, deterministic mechanics**. Mek provides a visual, pattern‑matching rule language (MekLang) and an execution sandbox that allows designers to rapidly prototype and analyze mechanics without building full games.

Mek’s design philosophy is complementary to Mortar’s. Mortar evolves mechanics algorithmically; Mek empowers designers to author mechanics directly. Both systems treat mechanics as **state‑transition rules**, but they differ in representation, purpose, and workflow.

---

## **Mek’s Representation of Mechanics**  
- MekLang syntax  
- tile‑pattern rules

MekLang expresses mechanics as **local tile‑pattern rules** of the form:

```
when <pattern>
do   <state changes>
```

Patterns describe spatial relationships around a center tile, and actions rewrite tile states to simulate movement, interaction, or transformation. Mek intentionally avoids sprite‑based motion or full game specification; instead, it focuses on **mechanics as atomic state transitions**, enabling rapid iteration and controlled experimentation.

This contrasts with Mortar’s representation of mechanics as **Python functions**, which are mutated by a large language model. Mek’s rules are human‑authored and visually structured; Mortar’s are machine‑generated and code‑level.

---

## **Mechanics vs Games: Shared Conceptual Foundation**  
- mechanics vs games

Both Mek and Mortar adopt a strict separation between:

- **mechanics** → rules that define state transitions  
- **games** → sets of mechanics + termination conditions  

Mek uses this distinction to support **mechanics‑first prototyping**, while Mortar uses it to support **mechanics‑first evolution**. In both systems, mechanics are treated as modular, composable units that can be combined into larger rule sets.

---

## **Complementarity with Mortar’s Pipeline**  
- mechanic evolution  
- MAP‑Elites

Mortar’s pipeline—LLM mutation, MAP‑Elites search, MCTS game assembly, and skill‑ordering evaluation—operates on mechanics expressed as code. Mek provides a **human‑interpretable DSL** that could serve as:

- a **front‑end** for human designers to seed Mortar’s mechanic archive  
- a **visual debugging layer** for inspecting evolved mechanics  
- a **constraint language** for restricting the search space  
- a **ground truth** for studying mechanic expressiveness and equivalence  

Mortar’s mechanics are often difficult to interpret due to LLM mutation and code‑level variation. Mek’s visual rule structure offers a way to analyze or categorize mechanics in terms of spatial patterns, interaction motifs, or tile‑level semantics.

---

## **Potential Integration: Mek as a Human‑Readable Layer**  
- LLM mutation constraints  
- behavior descriptors

A future hybrid system could translate Mortar‑evolved mechanics into MekLang patterns, enabling:

- **human inspection** of evolved rules  
- **visual debugging** of mechanic behavior  
- **pattern‑level descriptors** for QD feature maps  
- **rule‑level constraints** to guide LLM mutation  

Mortar’s evaluation pipeline could also be applied to MekLang mechanics directly, allowing designers to test whether their rules produce games with strong skill gradients.

---

## Summary
: Mek’s Role in Automatic Game Design Research

Mek represents the **human‑authored, visual DSL** side of mechanics research. Mortar represents the **machine‑generated, evolutionary search** side. Both systems treat mechanics as first‑class objects and emphasize modularity, expressiveness, and state‑transition semantics.

Together, they outline a spectrum:

| System | Purpose | Representation | Workflow |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mek** | Human prototyping | Visual tile‑pattern DSL | Manual rule design + sandbox testing |
| **Mortar** | Automatic mechanic evolution | Python functions mutated by LLM | QD search + MCTS + agent evaluation |

Mortar could benefit from Mek’s clarity; Mek could benefit from Mortar’s generative power. Their combination suggests a future in which designers and algorithms co‑create mechanics, each contributing strengths the other lacks.
