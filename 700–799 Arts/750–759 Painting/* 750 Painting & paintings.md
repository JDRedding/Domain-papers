# Painting (General)
- 750 Painting & paintings

Painting is one of the most durable ways people have given form to seeing, believing, and remembering. It is never only a surface of color. It is a meeting of method and meaning: the physical facts of pigment and support, the grammar of hue and light, and the stories a culture tells about the sacred, the ordinary, and the self. Across periods and places, those elements change in emphasis, but they remain bound together.

Painting joins craft and meaning. Technique, support, color, and form carry symbolism and story, from the sacred to the ordinary. Skill and idea work together when material and image support layered sense. Taken together, painting is a practice in which craft and concept cannot be cleanly split. The viscosity of oil, the stain of watercolor, the glow of gold, the cold of a complementary shadow, the tilt of a hand, the presence of a skull at the edge of a feast—all of these are technical decisions that are also decisions about meaning. The range of subjects, from altar to market stall, from hero to fruit on a plate, shows how wide human concern is. Mastery is not the accumulation of tricks. It is the ability to make material, color, and image speak in one voice, so that what is seen is also what is meant.

## 1. Techniques, Equipment, Materials & Forms

Painting uses many media: oil, watercolor, tempera, fresco, acrylic, encaustic, and gouache. Methods such as glazing, impasto, scumbling, alla prima, grisaille, sfumato, and *alla maniera antica* change texture, transparency, and depth.

The first fact of painting is material. Oil, watercolor, tempera, fresco, acrylic, encaustic, and gouache each dry, stain, and layer differently. Glazing builds transparent depth; impasto raises paint into relief; scumbling breaks color across a surface; alla prima keeps the mark wet and immediate; grisaille and sfumato model form through tone and haze. The tool is part of the thought: a stiff brush cuts an edge, a palette knife scrapes a plane, a sponge blurs a sky, an airbrush veils a transition, a tablet translates gesture into pixel. Canvas, wood, paper, plaster, and gilded metal are not neutral grounds. They absorb, resist, gleam, or crack, and the painter works with that resistance. Pigments—mineral, organic, synthetic—and mediums such as linseed oil or acrylic gel decide how long a passage stays open and how it will age. From these choices two large formal families emerge: images that point to the visible world and images that refuse it. Portrait, landscape, still life, narrative panel, and collage are not merely subjects; they are habits of looking.

Tools include brushes of different stiffness and shape, palette knives, sponges, airbrushes, and digital tablets. Supports range from canvas and wood panels to paper, plaster, and gilded metal. Pigments may be mineral, organic, or synthetic; binders such as linseed oil or acrylic gels control handling and drying.

Two broad formal categories are representational and abstract. Common types include portrait, landscape, still life, narrative panels, and collage.

## 2. Color

Color works aesthetically and symbolically. Hue, saturation, and value set mood, space, and emphasis. Color theory (primaries, secondaries, complements; warm vs. cool palettes) shapes emotional tone. Conventional associations include red with passion or danger, blue with calm, white with purity, black with mourning, and gold with the sacred.

Color is both structure and speech. Hue, saturation, and value create space, mood, and focus. Complementary contrasts sharpen a figure; a cool field can push a warm accent forward; a restricted palette can bind a scene into one climate of feeling. Color also carries convention. Red has long been used for passion, danger, or liturgical importance; blue for distance, stillness, or heaven; white for purity or absence; black for mourning or void; gold for uncreated light. These associations are not laws, but they are a language viewers already half know, and painters use or break that language with purpose.

## 3. Symbolism, Allegory, Mythology & Legend

Objects, colors, and gestures can stand for ideas (a skull as *memento mori*, an hourglass as time). Allegory personifies virtues, vices, or social ideals. Myth and legend—Greek, Roman, Norse, Hindu, and local—supply gods, heroes, and scenes that artists reuse for devotion, instruction, or commentary.

Meaning thickens when objects and bodies stand for more than themselves. A skull, an hourglass, a snuffed candle, a wilting flower can turn a still life into a meditation on time. Allegory dresses virtues and vices as persons or beasts so that an ethical argument can be seen. Myth and legend—Greek and Roman cycles, Norse sagas, Hindu epics, local saints and spirits—give painters a ready theater of gods, heroes, and trials. To paint a myth is rarely only to illustrate a tale; it is often to recast that tale for a new public, a new faith, or a new political claim.

## 4. Genre Paintings

Genre scenes show everyday life: taverns, interiors, markets, festivals. They often comment on social habit or morality rather than on grand history or scripture.

Genre painting looks the other way: toward kitchens, taverns, markets, streets, and festivals. Its ambition is not the miracle or the battle but the pattern of ordinary life. That pattern is never innocent. A tavern scene can moralize or merely observe; a domestic interior can honor labor or expose constraint. Genre is history without the official title, social study without the treatise.

## 5. Religion

Sacred painting uses iconography, perspective, and scale to mark the holy. Examples include Christian altarpieces, Islamic calligraphy and geometry, Hindu cosmic deities, and Buddhist mandalas.

Religious painting makes the invisible visible under rules of reverence. Christian altarpieces arrange saints and donors in a hierarchy of scale and light. Islamic traditions often turn from the figure toward calligraphy and geometry that suggest order beyond the eye. Hindu images place deities in cosmic settings that are at once narrative and metaphysical. Buddhist mandalas diagram the path of mind as much as they decorate a wall. In each case, technique serves doctrine: gold leaf, frontal pose, radial composition, or vast emptiness are not decorations added after the fact. They are the theology, made optical.

## 6. Human Figures

The figure is central in most traditions. Artists treat anatomy, proportion, gesture, and expression in styles from classical realism to abstraction. Portraits signal status, identity, or allegory; groups can stage history, ritual, or moral drama.

The human figure remains the most persistent problem and prize. Anatomy, proportion, gesture, and expression decide whether a body looks observed, idealized, or invented. A portrait may record a face, a rank, a profession, or an inner claim the sitter wishes to leave behind. Group compositions turn individuals into an event: a council, a sacrament, a massacre, a wedding. Abstraction does not abolish the figure so much as test how little of it is needed for recognition and feeling to survive.

## 7. Other Subjects

Landscape may be idealized or observed. Still life often turns on abundance, decay, or *memento mori*. Animals, architecture, and purely abstract design are also major subjects.

Landscape can be a window onto a real place or a constructed paradise. Still life can celebrate plenty or insist that plenty ends. Animals carry emblem and appetite. Architecture offers measured streets, ruins, temples, or impossible cities. Abstract painting withdraws from nameable things in order to work with rhythm, interval, and force alone. None of these subjects is lesser by nature; each asks a different kind of attention.

## 8. Historical, Geographic & Persons Treatment

History painting dramatizes war, nation, and mythic past. Landscape and cityscape locate culture in place. Portraiture records rank, character, and memory.

History painting claims public memory. Wars, foundations, treaties, and martyrdoms are staged so that a community can see what it thinks it was. Geography enters as landmark, climate, and local light: a mountain range, a harbor, a particular tree. Persons enter as names that painting keeps from vanishing. The historical picture is rarely a neutral document. It selects, heightens, and moralizes. That is its power and its danger.
