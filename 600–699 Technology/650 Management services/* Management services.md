# Management and Auxiliary Services
- 650 Management services

**Management services** is the field concerned with planning, organizing, leading, and controlling work, together with the office, communication, accounting, and promotional services that make organized activity possible. It covers both **general management**—the direction of people and resources toward goals—and the **supporting crafts** of the office: records, correspondence, shorthand, bookkeeping, and public communication. **Management services** are the organized effort to direct work and to support that direction with offices, writing, records of speech, accounts, and public communication. General management sets and pursues goals. Office services, written communication, and shorthand carry and store the work. Accounting measures it. Advertising and public relations explain it to markets and publics. Together they are the infrastructure of coordinated human activity.

The field is practical and applied. It treats organizations of every kind—firms, nonprofits, government offices, and households run as enterprises—and asks how work is designed, recorded, financed, and explained to the public.

## Scope of the Field

| Area | Concern |
| --- | --- |
| Office services | Space, equipment, records, and clerical systems that keep an organization running |
| Written communication | Methods of producing, transmitting, and storing business writing |
| Shorthand | Fast notation for dictation and minutes |
| Accounting | Recording, measuring, and reporting financial activity |
| General management | Planning, organizing, staffing, leading, and control |
| Advertising and public relations | Paid and unpaid communication with markets and publics |

These parts depend on one another. Management sets aims; the office and the accounts make those aims visible; writing and shorthand carry instruction; advertising and public relations present the organization to the outside.

## How the Parts Fit Together

A decision at the top (general management) is written (communication, sometimes from shorthand notes), recorded in the books (accounting), filed and scheduled (office services), and, when it concerns the outside world, announced or promoted (advertising and PR). Weakness in any one part shows up in the others: sloppy minutes produce sloppy accounts; unclear ads produce complaints the office cannot answer.

Digital tools have compressed these trades—email instead of the typing pool, dashboards instead of bound ledgers, content calendars instead of paste-up rooms—but the functions remain.

## Office Services

**Office services** are the systems and labor that support administration: reception, filing, mail, scheduling, supplies, and information handling.

A well-run office:

- defines who owns which records and tasks
- standardizes forms, templates, and filing (paper or digital)
- controls correspondence and appointments
- maintains equipment, software, and supplies
- protects confidentiality and retention rules

Modern office work is as much information work as paper work. Document management, calendars, shared drives, and customer databases have taken over many older clerical routines, but the principles remain: accuracy, traceability, and economy of motion.

## Processes of Written Communication

**Written communication** in organizations includes letters, memoranda, reports, email, minutes, proposals, and policy texts. The process typically runs from purpose and audience, through drafting and review, to transmission, filing, and reply.

Effective business writing is:

- **Clear.** One main point; short sentences; concrete verbs
- **Complete.** Facts the reader needs, and no more
- **Correct.** Grammar, names, figures, and legal wording
- **Courteous.** Tone fitted to rank and relationship
- **Current.** Dated, versioned, and stored where others can find it

Formats matter because they signal role: a contract is not a chat message. House style, templates, and approval paths reduce error and protect the organization.

## Shorthand

**Shorthand** is a system of abbreviated writing used to capture speech at speed—dictation, meetings, interviews, and court or legislative record.

Historic systems include Pitman, Gregg, and Teeline. They substitute signs or shortened outlines for sounds, words, or phrases. In many offices, shorthand has been partly replaced by recording, speech recognition, and keyboard skill, but the underlying need remains: a faithful, timely record of what was said.

Related skills are note-taking, minute-writing, and transcription. The test of the record is not speed alone but accuracy and usefulness to those who were not in the room.

## Accounting

**Accounting** is the measurement and communication of economic activity. It turns transactions into statements that managers, owners, lenders, and regulators can use.

Core work includes:

- **Bookkeeping.** Journals, ledgers, and the double-entry rule (every debit has a credit)
- **Financial accounting.** Balance sheet, income statement, and cash-flow statement for outside users
- **Management accounting.** Costs, budgets, and variances for internal decision
- **Audit and internal control.** Checks that figures can be trusted
- **Tax and compliance.** Reports required by law

Accounting does not merely count money. It assigns events to periods, distinguishes asset from expense, and makes performance comparable over time. Without it, planning and control have no measure.

## General Management

**General management** is the coordination of people and resources to reach stated aims efficiently and effectively. It applies in business, public administration, and nonprofit work.

### Functions

- **Planning.** Objectives, forecasts, and strategy
- **Organizing.** Structure, roles, and assignment of work
- **Staffing.** Recruitment, training, and placement
- **Leading.** Direction, motivation, and communication
- **Controlling.** Standards, measurement, and correction

### Levels

- **Top management.** Policy and long direction (boards, chief officers)
- **Middle management.** Translation of strategy into departmental plans
- **First-line management.** Day-to-day supervision of work

### Styles and skills

Styles range from autocratic (useful in crisis) through democratic and coaching forms to laissez-faire (fit for skilled, self-directed teams). Transformational leadership tries to raise performance by appealing to purpose, not only to rule.

Needed skills mix the technical (the work of the unit), the conceptual (the whole organization), and the interpersonal (talk, conflict, and trust). Time, delegation, and decision under uncertainty are part of the craft.

Management is both method and judgment. Charts and metrics do not replace the duty to choose, explain, and answer for results.

## Advertising and Public Relations

**Advertising** is paid, controlled communication meant to inform or persuade a market—goods, services, ideas, or candidates. It uses space and time in media, and now also search, social platforms, and direct digital channels. Typical work includes research, positioning, copy and design, media buying, and measurement of response.

**Public relations (PR)** is the managed relationship between an organization and its publics: customers, staff, investors, press, regulators, and neighbors. It includes news, events, issues management, and reputation. Unlike advertising, PR does not always buy the channel; it seeks credibility through third parties and consistent conduct.

Both rest on a brief that answers: Who must be reached? What should they think or do? What proof can be offered? How will effect be judged? Ethics matter: claims must be supportable, and silence or spin in a crisis often costs more than candor.

Advertising and PR serve management; they do not replace the quality of the product or the fairness of the firm.
