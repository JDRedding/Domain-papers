# **Strike‑plate fix**
Interior door knob latch

  Complete & Robust Door‑Frame Repair Summary (Strike‑Plate Impact Failure)

Rebuild the jamb section with long screws —  just need the latch to have a little breathing room.

---

## **1. Failure Mode Overview**
- Impact load caused **shear + splinter failure** of the strike‑plate side of the jamb.  
- Door slab is intact; damage is **localized to jamb face + latch zone**.  
- Repair requires **cut‑out, structural patch, hardware re‑mortise, and reinforcement**.

---

## **2. Critical Geometry (Must Be Preserved)**
- **Hinge leaf**: ~1½″ × 3½″.  
- **Strike‑plate face → door stop offset**: ~1½″ (matches 1⅜″ door thickness).  
- **Door stop projection**: ~½–¾″.  
Maintaining these ensures latch alignment, proper closure, and flush hardware seating.

---

## **3. Extraction & Cut‑Out**
- Remove strike plate and loose fibers.  
- Score paint lines for clean edges.  
- Mark a rectangular cutout from solid wood below the break to **6″+ above latch bore**.  
- Include full jamb face under the door stop.  
- Cut with oscillating tool; avoid penetrating the stud.

---

## **4. Structural Prep**
- Clear debris, nails, crushed fibers.  
- If cavity is soft or deep:  
  - Inject **two‑part structural epoxy** **or**  
  - Glue in a **hardwood reinforcement block**.  
This restores compressive strength behind the patch.

---

## **5. Patch Fabrication & Installation**
- Mill patch from matching jamb stock (1× pine or similar).  
- Dry‑fit for zero‑gap seating.  
- Glue edges + back with wood glue or construction adhesive.  
- Fasten with finish nails or countersunk screws.  
- Plane/sand flush to original jamb face.

---

## **6. Hardware Re‑Mortise**
- Use strike plate as template.  
- Bore latch hole with **1″ spade bit**.  
- Mortise plate recess with chisel or multi‑tool.  
- Test door closure for latch alignment.


### 🔧 Core takeaway  
For an **interior door knob latch**, the hole behind the strike plate is just a **shallow 1‑inch relief pocket**.  
**Do not** drill through the jamb.  
**Do not** need a deep deadbolt-style cavity.  
A **1‑inch spade bit** is the correct tool.

---

### 🪵 Why 1 inch matters  
The latch bolt head is roughly 1 inch wide, so the *diameter* of the relief pocket must match.  
Depth is minimal — the bolt only needs a little extra room to fully extend.

---

### 📐 Correct depth  

- **⅛ inch**: usually enough  
- **¼ inch**: if the latch still doesn’t fully throw  
- **½ inch max**: only if absolutely needed  
- **Never** drill through the jamb into the stud cavity

Interior knobs have short latch throws, so a shallow divot is all that’s required.

---

### 🛠 How to drill it cleanly  
- Mark the center of the strike opening  
- Remove the strike plate  
- Use a **1‑inch spade bit**  
- Run the drill **slow**  
- Create a shallow recess  
- Test latch throw  
- Deepen only if needed

---

### 🧱 Why you don’t drill all the way through  
People sometimes drill through because they think it’s like a deadbolt pocket.  
But drilling through:
- Weakens the jamb  
- Removes wood the strike screws need  
- Lets the latch over‑travel and bind  
- Causes sloppy latch engagement

---

## **7. Reinforced Strike‑Plate Installation**
- Install upgraded **heavy‑duty/security strike plate**.  
- Use **3″ screws** penetrating:  
  **patch → jamb → wall stud**.  
This creates a structural load path stronger than factory.

---

## **8. Finish & Cosmetic Restoration**
- Replace damaged door‑stop segment if needed.  
- Fill nail holes/gaps with wood filler.  
- Sand, caulk seams, prime, paint.  
- Final result is visually seamless and structurally superior.

---

## **9. Operator Notes**
- Maintain exact **strike‑plate face → door‑stop offset**.  
- Align latch with door **closed** during final fitting.  
- Hands‑on time: **1–2 hours**, plus adhesive cure + paint time.  
- Reinforced screws make the repaired zone **stronger than original**.

### 🔩 Summary
Replaced the blown‑out jamb section, anchored it with 3‑inch screws, got it flush, and now need the relief pocket behind the strike plate.  
A **1‑inch spade bit** is exactly the right tool, used **shallow**, not deep.
