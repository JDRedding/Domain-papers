MONIAC, also known as the Phillips Hydraulic Computer or Monetary National Income Analogue Computer, was an innovative analog machine that modeled national economic flows using water to simulate money circulation and national income. MONIAC stands out as one of the most imaginative analog computers ever built — a hydraulic macroeconomic simulator that used **water** to model **money**, **income flows**, and **policy effects** in real time. In summary, MONIAC translated the abstract ideas of macroeconomic flows into a visual, operable hydraulic system, highlighting Bill Phillips’ innovative approach to economics and analog computing.

## 📜 Origins and Purpose  
- Invented in **1949** by **Bill Phillips**, a New Zealand economist studying at the London School of Economics.   
- Built initially for **£400** using **Lancaster bomber parts** scavenged from war surplus. 
- Intended as a **teaching device**, but quickly recognized as a **functional economic simulator**.  
- Demonstrated to LSE economists in 1949, earning Phillips a teaching position.   

## 🧩 What MONIAC Actually Was  
- **Hydraulic analog computing** — It used continuously varying water levels to represent economic variables.  
- **Fluidic logic** — Tanks, pipes, floats, counterweights, and pumps implemented the “logic” of macroeconomic relationships.  
- **Macroeconomic simulation** — Consumption, taxation, savings, investment, imports, and exports were all represented as physical flows.  
- **Keynesian modeling** — The machine visually demonstrated Keynesian income‑expenditure dynamics.  

MONIAC is a rare example of **engineering meeting economics** in a way that is both **scientific** and **visually intuitive**. It demonstrates that before digital computation, economists could build **physical analogues** of entire national economies — and make them work.

## ⚙️ Core   
MONIAC was a **2‑meter‑tall hydraulic analog computer** built in 1949 by Bill Phillips to simulate the UK national economy using **fluidic logic**, transparent tanks, pumps, and valves. It was both a **teaching tool** and a surprisingly **accurate economic model**, achieving about **2% calibration accuracy**.   

## Overview and Purpose
MONIAC was invented in 1949 by economist Bill Phillips at the London School of Economics. Its primary purpose was to demonstrate and simulate the dynamics of a national economy, including consumption, investment, taxation, and government spending. By using water as a physical analogue for money, it visually and quantitatively illustrated economic concepts such as the circular flow of income, monetary supply, and fiscal policy effects.

### ⚙️ How MONIAC Worked  
- **Treasury tank** at the top represented government funds.  
- **Sector tanks** represented health, education, investment, savings, imports/exports, etc. 
- **Valves** adjusted tax rates, savings rates, and spending priorities.  
- **Pumps** returned water to the treasury to simulate taxation.  
- **Floats and electrodes** automatically triggered flows when levels reached thresholds.  
- **Graphing pens** recorded results on paper using a 1‑RPM motor.  

This made MONIAC one of the earliest **interactive macroeconomic models**, decades before digital computers could visualize such systems.

### Function and Operation
Hydraulic Analogues: Water represented the flow of money through an economy. Tanks corresponded to economic sectors—households, firms, government—and pipes and pumps represented spending, taxation, savings, and investments.
Adjustable Parameters: Users could change tax rates, savings rates, and investment behavior using valves or sliders, affecting water flow throughout the system.

Simulation of Macroeconomic Dynamics: Changes in inputs like government spending or taxation caused an immediate visual response in water levels and flows, showing effects on national income, consumption, and production.

Output Interpretation: Gauges and floats provided measurements corresponding to economic indicators, allowing economists and students to compute national income, total expenditure, and other metrics in real time.

## 🎓 Significance 
 
- **Educational breakthrough** — Students could *see* the circular flow of income.  
- **Early systems modeling** — Demonstrated feedback loops, equilibrium, and disequilibrium physically.  
- **Precursor to digital macro models** — Anticipated modern computational economics.  
- **Surprising accuracy** — Phillips and Newlyn found it could be calibrated to within **2%**. 

Educational Tool: MONIAC served as an interactive teaching aid that made abstract concepts of macroeconomics tangible and intuitive.

Early Economic Modeling: It was one of the first analog computers used for economics, demonstrating that physical systems could simulate complex feedback interactions in a national economy.

Influence on Later Models: MONIAC anticipated the use of digital computers for policy analysis and forecasting decades later, paving the way for computer-assisted economic modeling.

## 🏛️ Legacy and Surviving Units 
Several MONIAC units were constructed, and some survive in museums, particularly at the London School of Economics. While no longer practical for computation, it remains an iconic example of analog computing and a creative intersection of engineering and economics. Its visual demonstration continues to inspire both economists and educators in communicating economic dynamics effectively.
 
- About **12 MONIACs** were built and distributed to universities and central banks worldwide.   
- Working units survive at:
  - London School of Economics  
  - Reserve Bank of New Zealand Museum  
  - University of Melbourne (partially restored)  
- The only working MONIAC in the southern hemisphere is at the **Reserve Bank Museum**.
