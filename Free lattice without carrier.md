# Free lattice without carrier
**Point-free, purely syntactic construction** 

The lattice emerges entirely from relational axioms rather than point-set substrates,  by treating generators as formal syntactic combinators and constructing the free object as a quotiented term algebra. This is the foundation of **pointless topology** (locales), where spaces are studied purely via their open-set algebra rather than underlying sets of points.

**Universal algebra** and **categorical logic** decouple algebraic structure from underlying set-theoretic carriers.

- The carrier of the free lattice (or free frame) is a set of equivalence classes of terms, not a set of geometric points that are later organised into a lattice of subsets or open sets.
- No evaluation or realisation map into a concrete power set or topological space is required for the free object itself to exist.
- The universal property characterises the object up to unique isomorphism solely in terms of lattice (or frame) homomorphisms; spatiality is optional extra structure that may or may not be present.

This is exactly the algebraic starting point used in pointless topology / locale theory: one works with the frame of “opens” as a primary object, and points (if they exist) appear later as completely prime filters or as frame homomorphisms into $\mathbf{2}$.

The same pattern extends immediately to any other algebraic theory (Heyting algebras, quantales, etc.). In each case one obtains a free object whose underlying set is generated by formal terms, not by an a-priori geometric carrier.

## Null generator:

- **Initial lattice $L$:** take it as a free lattice (or free frame) on a set of *relation‑combinator symbols*.
- **No carrier:** those symbols are not “elements of a world”; they’re just primitive operations in the theory.
- **Closure $G$:** acts on $L$; fixed points $\mathrm{Fix}(G)$ give you the emergent domain.

**Signature**  

$$
\Sigma = \{\wedge,\vee\} \qquad\text{(or $\Sigma=\{ \wedge,\vee,0,1\}$ for bounded lattices)}
$$

Equational theory $E$ consisting of the usual lattice identities:
- associativity, commutativity, idempotence of both operations,
- absorption laws,
- (optionally) the two identities that make $0$ and $1$ the bottom and top.

Signatures: Pick the operations and laws. 

- **Signature:**  
  **Meet:** $\wedge$, **join:** $\vee$, optionally $0,1$  
- **Equations:** commutativity, associativity, idempotence, absorption, etc.

No carrier $X$. Just an algebraic theory of lattices.

```
+-----------------------------------+
|       FORMAL GENERATORS           |
|        G = { g1, g2, ... }        |
+-----------------+-----------------+
                  |
                  v (Syntax Trees)
+-----------------------------------+
|          RAW TERM ALGEBRA         |
|   t ::= g | t1 ^ t2 | t1 v t2     |
+-----------------+-----------------+
                  |
                  v (Equational Theory)
+-----------------------------------+
|      AXIOMATIC QUOTIENT / =       |
|    Associativity, Absorption,     |
|    Commutativity, Idempotence     |
+-----------------+-----------------+
                  |
                  v
+-----------------------------------+
|        FREE LATTICE L(G)          |
|    (Purely Syntactic / Relational)|
+-----------------------------------+

```

**Term algebra**  
Let ${G}$ be any set of symbols (the “generators”). The set $T({G})$ of $\Sigma$-terms is defined inductively exactly as you wrote:

$$
t ::= g \mid t_1\wedge t_2 \mid t_1\vee t_2,\qquad g\in{G}.
$$

No evaluation map, no underlying set of points, no power-set construction—pure syntax.

Let ${G}$ be a set of **generators**, but treat them as *formal relation‑combinators*, not “elements of a set”.

- **Raw terms:** all finite expressions built from ${G}$ using $\wedge,\vee$:

$$
t ::= g \mid t \wedge t \mid t \vee t,\quad g \in {G}
$$

- This is just syntax — no points, no subsets.

### Impose the lattice axioms by quotienting

Define an equivalence relation $\equiv$ on terms:

- $t_1 \equiv t_2$ iff you can derive $t_1 = t_2$ from the lattice axioms (associativity, commutativity, idempotence, absorption, etc.).

Then take:

$$
L := \text{Terms} / \equiv
$$

with operations:

$$
[t_1] \wedge [t_2] := [t_1 \wedge t_2],\quad
[t_1] \vee [t_2] := [t_1 \vee t_2]
$$

This $L$ is a **free lattice** on ${G}$: every lattice map out of $L$ is uniquely determined by where it sends the generators.

**Congruence**  
Let $\equiv_E$ be the smallest congruence on $T({G})$ that contains all substitution instances of the identities in $E$. Equivalently, $t_1\equiv_E t_2$ if and only if the equational theory of lattices proves $t_1=t_2$. The quotient

$$
L = T({G})/{\equiv_E}
$$

carries well-defined operations

$$
[t_1]\wedge[t_2] := [t_1\wedge t_2],\qquad
[t_1]\vee[t_2] := [t_1\vee t_2]
$$

and is the free lattice on ${G}$. The universal property is the usual one: any set-function ${G}\to M$ into a lattice $M$ extends uniquely to a lattice homomorphism $L\to M$.

The generators themselves appear only as formal symbols. The map ${G}\hookrightarrow L$ that sends each generator to its equivalence class is injective (free lattices on any set of generators are known to embed the generators), but that embedding is a consequence of the construction, not an assumption that the generators already live inside some ambient set of “points.”

### Frames and the ideal monad

For frames the same pattern works, with two changes:

1. The signature is enlarged to allow arbitrary (set-indexed) joins.
2. The equational theory includes the infinite distributive law

$$
a\wedge\bigvee_i b_i = \bigvee_i (a\wedge b_i).
$$

Equivalently, start from a distributive lattice $D$ and form the frame ${Idl}(D)$ of its ideals ordered by inclusion. Joins are unions of ideals; finite meets are intersections of ideals. This construction is the free-frame functor on the category of distributive lattices (the “ideal lattice monad”). Again the construction is purely algebraic; no space of points is presupposed.

### Deep Perspective

To take this construction to its purest expression:

1. **Lawvere Theories (Functorial Semantics):** A lattice theory can be presented as a small category $\mathbb{T}_{\text{Lat}}$ with finite products, whose objects are the arities $[n]$. A lattice *without* an underlying set is simply a **product‑preserving functor**  

$$
\mathbb{T}_{\text{Lat}} \longrightarrow \mathbf{C},
$$

where $\mathbf{C}$ is any category with finite products—such as a topos or a symmetric monoidal category. In this formulation, the lattice is interpreted internally to $\mathbf{C}$, bypassing $\mathbf{Set}$ entirely.

2. **The Adjunction Perspective:** The syntactic free construction represents the left adjoint to the forgetful functor:

$${F} \dashv {U} : \mathbf{Lat} \to \mathbf{Set}$$

Because ${F}(G) \cong \text{Terms}(G)/{\equiv}$, the universal mapping property guarantees that for any lattice $A$ and any formal assignment $\alpha: G \to {U}(A)$, there exists a unique lattice homomorphism $\bar{\alpha}: L(G) \to A$ making the diagram commute:

```
        G ---- alpha ----> U(A)
        |                  ^
      eta                  | U(\bar{\alpha})
        v                  |
     U(L(G)) - U(\bar{\alpha}) -+

```

3. **Whitman's Solution & Canonical Word Forms:** In ordinary free lattices (non-distributive), deciding equivalence ($t_1 \equiv t_2$) is non-trivial. Whitman (1941) showed the word problem for free lattices is decidable by reducing order relations purely recursively on syntactic structure:
* $x \le y$ for $x, y \in G \iff x = y$
* $t_1 \vee t_2 \le u \iff t_1 \le u \text{ and } t_2 \le u$
* $u \le t_1 \wedge t_2 \iff u \le t_1 \text{ and } u \le t_2$
* $t_1 \wedge t_2 \le u_1 \vee u_2 \iff t_1 \le u_1 \vee u_2 \text{ or } t_2 \le u_1 \vee u_2 \text{ or } t_1 \wedge t_2 \le u_1 \text{ or } t_1 \wedge t_2 \le u_2$

4. **Point-Free Locales & the Ideal Completion:** When extended to frames ($\mathbf{Frm}$), the ideal lattice monad $\mathcal{I}$ embeds a bounded distributive lattice $\mathbf{DLat}$ into a frame where joins commute with finite meets:

$$\mathcal{I}(L) = \{ I \subseteq L \mid I \text{ is a downward-closed, directed-join closed ideal} \}$$
