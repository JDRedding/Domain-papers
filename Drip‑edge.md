## Drip‑edge

Rainwater tends to cling to surfaces because of **surface tension** and **adhesion**. On horizontal or near‑horizontal building elements, this causes water to travel backward toward walls, sills, and soffits instead of falling away. This leads to staining, moisture saturation, and long‑term material decay.

**Fundamental architectural response:**  
Across cultures and eras, builders introduced a **geometric discontinuity**—a sharp edge, groove, undercut, or projection—that **breaks the continuous water film**, forcing droplets to detach and fall clear of the structure. This detail is small but essential: it ensures water leaves the building rather than following its surfaces.

- Water follows continuous surfaces.  
- A deliberate **break in continuity** forces detachment.  
- This principle is invariant across materials, cultures, and eras.  
- The detail is small, but its absence reliably causes damage.  
- The solution has remained geometrically simple for over two millennia.

---

## **Historical pattern**

- **Classical architecture:**  
  Greek and Roman builders used projecting cornices, bird’s‑beak moldings, and stone grooves to interrupt water flow.

- **South Asian rock‑cut architecture:**  
  Sri Lankan cave temples (3rd century BCE onward) incorporated continuous carved drip ledges (katâraya) to keep monsoon runoff out of interior spaces.

- **Medieval Europe:**  
  Dripstones, hood‑moulds, and gargoyles served the same function—ornamented but fundamentally geometric drip edges.

- **Later periods:**  
  When these details were omitted or poorly executed, masonry staining and moisture damage occurred, often requiring metal retrofits.

- **Modern construction:**  
  Industrialized metal flashing standardized the drip‑edge concept. U.S. building codes formally required drip edges only starting in 2012. Contemporary stone and concrete work uses precision‑cut grooves to achieve the same effect.
