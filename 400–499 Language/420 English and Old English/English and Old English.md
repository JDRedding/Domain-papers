# English and Old English
- 420 English and Old English

Modern English descends from Old English, but the two are mutually unintelligible for most people without specialized study. Old English contributed core vocabulary—words such as *man*, *house*, *water*, and *strong*—and some syntactic patterns. Most abstract, legal, scholarly, and artistic vocabulary comes from later borrowings. Scholars study Old English to understand the history of the language and medieval literature.

Old English is the earliest phase of English, with complex grammar and mostly Germanic vocabulary. Modern English is a simplified descendant with extensive borrowings and sound changes produced by contact with other languages. The shift from one to the other shows how English has adapted over time. Old English is the early form of the English language spoken in England from roughly the 5th to the 12th century. Modern English is its later, evolved form, shaped by Norman French, Latin, and other languages. 

## Old English (c. 450–1150 CE)

Old English originated with the Germanic tribes—the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes—who migrated to England after the fall of the Roman Empire. It differs sharply from Modern English in vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation.

**Grammar.** Old English was highly inflected. Nouns, pronouns, and adjectives had case endings (nominative, accusative, genitive, dative), and verbs were conjugated extensively for person, number, tense, and mood.

**Vocabulary.** The lexicon was mostly Germanic and often unfamiliar to modern speakers. For example, *cyning* means “king,” and *hwæt* is an attention-getting particle meaning “lo” or “listen,” as in *Beowulf*: “Hwæt! We Gardena in geardagum…”

**Pronunciation.** It used sounds no longer found in the same way in Modern English, including þ (thorn) and ð (eth), both pronounced as “th.”

**Alphabet.** The writing system was primarily Latin-based but included characters such as þ (thorn), ð (eth), æ (ash), and ƿ (wynn).

**Literature.** Famous works include *Beowulf*, the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, and various religious texts.

## Middle English (c. 1150–1500 CE)

After the Norman Conquest in 1066, English absorbed large amounts of French and Latin vocabulary, and inflectional endings began to simplify. Grammar became less complex, and word order grew more rigid. Notable texts from this period include Chaucer’s *The Canterbury Tales*.

## Modern English (from c. 1500 CE)

Modern English developed with simplified grammar and a greatly expanded vocabulary drawn from Latin, Greek, French, Dutch, and other languages.

**Grammar.** Most case endings disappeared, and verb conjugations were simplified. Word order became the main way of showing grammatical relationships.

**Vocabulary.** The lexicon combines Germanic roots from Old English with extensive later borrowings.

**Pronunciation.** The Great Vowel Shift (c. 1400–1700) changed the pronunciation of long vowels and helped produce a system closer to today’s English.

**Writing system.** English adopted the standardized Latin alphabet (A–Z) and dropped Old English characters.
