# Mathematicall Magick
- 510 Mathematics, John Wilkins’ historical treatise on mathematical devices
- 620 Mechanics, Wilkins’ historical treatise on mechanical devices

**Mathematicall Magick** is a 1648 two-book treatise by John Wilkins that surveys classical mechanical devices and explores speculative technologies such as flying machines, submarines, automata, and perpetual motion. Wilkins’s treatise blends classical mechanics, early engineering, and speculative technology, illustrating the 17th-century transition from Renaissance natural philosophy to systematic scientific inquiry. It also shows Wilkins’s broader goal: to popularize scientific knowledge and encourage experimentation.

## Overview
### Publication History
Wilkins’s *Mathematicall Magick. Or, The Wonders that may be Performed by Mechanicall Geometry* was first published in London in 1648. Later editions appeared in 1680, 1691, and 1707. The work was printed for Samuel Gellibrand and dedicated to Charles Louis, Elector Palatine. Surviving copies include the first edition (available on the Internet Archive) and the 1691 fourth edition (held in collections such as the Sir John Soane’s Museum).

Wilkins—an English clergyman, natural philosopher, and polymath—intended the treatise to make mechanical principles accessible and to encourage scientific study.

### Structure of the Work
Wilkins divides the treatise into two books, each named after a classical figure:

- Book I: “Archimedes”—because Archimedes was “chiefest in discovering of Mechanical powers.”
- Book II: “Daedalus”—referencing the legendary inventor associated with automata and flight.

### Book I: Classical Mechanical Powers
The first book contains 20 chapters covering traditional mechanical devices and principles:

- The balance, lever, wheel/pulley, block and tackle, wedge, and screw.
- Comparisons between mechanical forces and those acting in the human body.
- Discussion of Archimedes’ famous claim that, with a proper fulcrum, he could “move the world.”
- Explanations of gear trains and how linked transmissions can theoretically increase speed beyond Earth’s rotational speed.
- Comparisons of siege engines (e.g., catapults) with the cost and effectiveness of 17th-century guns.

A portion of this material survives in digitized form, such as the EEBO transcription of Chapter XII on balance and lever mechanics.

### Book II: Automata, Submarines, Flying Machines, and Perpetual Motion
The second book contains 15 chapters exploring devices that move without direct human force:

- Clocks, watches, water mills, windmills, and devices driven by airflow or pressurized air.
- Designs for land yachts with sails and wagons powered by vertical-axis wind turbines.
- Descriptions of small automata—figures of men and animals that move independently.
- Improvements to Cornelis Drebbel’s submarine, one of the earliest practical submersibles.

### Wilkins on Human Flight
Wilkins devotes two chapters to the possibility of human flight, arguing that it is achievable with sufficient experimentation. He recounts early flight attempts:

- Eilmer of Malmesbury, an 11th-century monk who reportedly glided “above a furlong.”
- Fausto Veranzio, who attempted a parachute-like descent from St. Mark’s steeple in Venice.
- A Turkish experimenter in Constantinople described by Busbecq.

Wilkins proposes:

- Wings attached to the body, though he doubts their practicality.
- A “flying chariot”—a frame carrying a person, possibly large enough for multiple people working in succession to keep it aloft.

He argues that doubts about such machines can be overcome through systematic development and testing.

### Perpetual Motion and Perpetual Lamps
Chapters IX–XV discuss:

- The theoretical feasibility of perpetual motion.
- Stories of lamps burning for centuries and how such devices might be constructed.

These sections reflect early modern curiosity rather than modern physical understanding.
