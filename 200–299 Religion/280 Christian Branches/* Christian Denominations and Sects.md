# Christian Denominations and Sects
- 280 Christian Sects

Christianity is divided into many denominations and movements. They are usually grouped under three major branches—Roman Catholicism, Eastern Orthodoxy, and Protestantism—together with smaller and independent groups. Christianity is highly diverse. It ranges from large, structured communions such as Catholicism and Orthodoxy to independent groups with distinctive beliefs and practices. Denominations display a wide spectrum of faith and theological interpretation shaped over many centuries. In short: **denominations** are established streams of Christianity with recognized structures; **sects** are protest or restoration groups defined by sharper boundaries. Many of today’s denominations began as sects.


## Denominations vs. Sects

In everyday speech, **denomination** and **sect** are often used loosely for any distinct Christian group. In history and the sociology of religion they are not synonyms. The difference is mainly one of relation to the surrounding church and society, not of whether a group is “real” Christianity.

### Denomination

A **denomination** is a relatively stable, organized body within a larger religious tradition. Typical marks include:

- recognized standing among other churches
- a defined creed, liturgy, or confession, with room for internal variety
- formal structures (synods, bishops, conventions, congregations)
- an accepted place in public life—schools, charities, chaplaincies, civic partnership
- membership that is often inherited as well as chosen

Catholicism, Eastern Orthodoxy, Lutheranism, Anglicanism, Methodism, and the major Baptist conventions are usually described as denominations (or families of denominations). A denomination claims to represent the Christian faith but does not, in modern usage, deny that other churches may also be Christian.

### Sect

A **sect**, in the classic sociological sense (Ernst Troeltsch, H. Richard Niebuhr, and later writers), is a group that has broken from a parent church in the name of purity, restoration, or intensified commitment. Typical marks include:

- origin in protest or schism
- stricter boundaries of belief and behavior
- voluntary, often adult, membership
- tension with the majority church or with the surrounding culture
- a claim to recover the “true” or original faith

Anabaptist groups in the sixteenth century, early Methodism before it became a denomination, and many Holiness or fundamentalist breakaways began as sects in this sense. Over time a sect may soften, grow institutions, and become a denomination—a common path in Christian history.

### How the Terms Are Used in Practice

| | Denomination | Sect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relation to other churches | One recognized body among others | Sharper break; often exclusive claims |
| Organization | Established offices and institutions | Simpler, tighter, sometimes charismatic leadership |
| Society | Usually accommodated to public life | Higher tension with culture or the state |
| Membership | Often mixed inherited and chosen | Typically voluntary and demanding |
| Tone of the word | Neutral or official | Neutral in scholarship; sometimes pejorative in popular speech |

Because “sect” can sound dismissive, many writers prefer **movement**, **communion**, **tradition**, or **new religious movement** unless they are using the sociological contrast on purpose. Groups such as Jehovah’s Witnesses or the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints are variously called denominations, sects, or restorationist churches, depending on whether the speaker stresses organization, tension with historic Christianity, or self-description.

### Why the Distinction Matters

Calling a body a denomination usually locates it inside the historic family of churches and the ordinary ecumenical conversation. Calling it a sect highlights protest, separation, and a tighter demand on members. Neither label by itself settles questions of truth, salvation, or orthodoxy; it only describes how a group stands toward other Christians and toward the wider society.

## Major Branches

### 1. Roman Catholicism

The largest Christian communion, Roman Catholicism emphasizes the authority of the pope, a sacramental system (including baptism, the Eucharist, and confession), and the authority of church tradition together with the Bible. It is centered on the Vatican and has a hierarchical clergy. It is prominent in Europe, the Americas, and parts of Africa and Asia.

### 2. Eastern Orthodoxy

Eastern Orthodox churches stress continuity with the early Church, the liturgy, and the sacraments. National churches (for example, the Russian and Greek Orthodox churches) are autocephalous. The Patriarch of Constantinople is regarded as “first among equals,” not as a supreme pontiff. Theology and practice are closely tied to the Nicene Creed and the early ecumenical councils.

### 3. Protestantism

Protestant denominations arose in the sixteenth-century Reformation, in challenge to certain Catholic doctrines. They typically emphasize *sola scriptura* (the Bible as the primary authority) and justification by faith. Main families include:

- **Lutheranism.** Follows Martin Luther’s teaching and sacramental practice.
- **Anglicanism / Episcopalianism.** Holds together elements of Catholic tradition and Reformation principle.
- **Reformed / Presbyterian.** Emphasizes Reformed doctrine, including predestination, and government by elders.
- **Baptists.** Stress believer’s baptism and congregational government.
- **Methodists.** Emphasize personal piety, social holiness, and a connectional church structure.
- **Pentecostalism.** Highlights gifts of the Holy Spirit, including speaking in tongues and healing.

## Smaller Movements and Groups

Beyond these branches are many smaller and independent movements, often marked by theological, social, or cultural distinctives:

- **Adventists** (for example, Seventh-day Adventists): the nearness of the Second Coming and Saturday Sabbath observance.
- **Jehovah’s Witnesses:** distinctive teaching about the Kingdom of God and the rejection of traditional Christian holidays.
- **The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Mormonism):** additional scripture, including the Book of Mormon.
- **Anabaptists** (for example, Mennonites and Amish): adult baptism, simplicity, and often pacifism.

Some of these groups stand at the edge of, or outside, the historic Protestant family; they are listed here as part of the wider Christian landscape rather than as standard Protestant denominations.

## Ecumenical Perspective

Despite deep differences, many churches take part in ecumenical dialogue and cooperation. Christians differ widely in liturgy, government, doctrine, and culture, yet commonly confess Jesus Christ, the resurrection, and the moral teaching of the faith.
