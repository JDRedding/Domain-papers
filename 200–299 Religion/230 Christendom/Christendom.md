**Christendom** refers to the collective society, culture, and political structures in which Christianity was the defining religious and cultural influence, especially in Europe from the early Middle Ages through the early modern period.

## Definition and Scope

Christendom is not merely the community of Christians. It names a broader civilizational and political order in which Christianity shaped law, culture, politics, and social norms. Historically the term most often describes European societies united under Christian influence, encompassing both the Eastern (Byzantine) and Western (Latin) churches. It can also include the wider world shaped by European Christian expansion, particularly during the colonial era.

## Historical Development

**Early Christendom (4th–6th centuries).** After Constantine the Great legalized Christianity in the Roman Empire through the Edict of Milan (313 CE), and after Christianity became the state religion by the end of the fourth century, rulers and the Church worked together to build a Christian political and cultural order.

**Medieval Christendom (6th–15th centuries).** In the Middle Ages, Christendom was defined by the interplay of monarchs and the Church. Popes, bishops, and monasteries held substantial social, political, and economic power. Christianity shaped law, education, art, and architecture—visible in cathedrals, scholarly institutions, and the unifying role of the Latin Church in Western Europe.

**High Middle Ages and the Crusades.** Christendom expanded territorially and ideologically through events such as the Crusades, which expressed a shared Christian identity and a readiness to wage war in defense or promotion of the faith.

**Late medieval to early modern period.** The authority of Christendom came under strain from emerging nation-states, the Protestant Reformation, and internal church conflicts. Even so, its cultural and ethical frameworks continued to shape law, morality, and international relations.

## Characteristics of Christendom

- **Cultural unity.** Shared beliefs, liturgy, festivals, and moral frameworks fostered a common identity across diverse regions.
- **Political integration.** Monarchs often claimed legitimacy through the Church, and the papacy exercised influence over kings and emperors.
- **Educational influence.** The Church controlled most schools and universities, preserving literacy, scholarship, and classical learning through monasteries and universities.
- **Military and expansionist activity.** Christendom sometimes pursued religious wars, missionary work, and colonization, joining religious ideals to political and cultural ambitions.

## Enduring Influence

Even after medieval Christendom ceased to function as a unified political-religious entity, the concept continued to shape Western legal systems, social norms, artistic achievements, and the global spread of Christianity. It remains useful in historical and cultural study as a name for a period in which religion, governance, and daily life were tightly interwoven.

In essence, Christendom denotes a historical civilization defined by Christian faith and by that faith’s comprehensive effect on society, culture, and politics.
