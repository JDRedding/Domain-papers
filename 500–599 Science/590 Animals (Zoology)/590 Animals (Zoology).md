#  Zoology
- Animals (Kingdom Animalia)
- 590 Animals (Zoology)

In zoology, animals multicellular, heterotrophic eukaryotes in the kingdom **Animalia** (Metazoa). They obtain energy by consuming other organisms and share a common evolutionary ancestry marked by specialized tissues, motility at some life stage, and a conserved developmental program that includes blastulation and gastrulation. Zoology studies animals to understand:

- Evolutionary relationships and the origin of body plans
- Behavior and cognition
- Anatomy and physiology
- Ecological roles and trophic dynamics
- Conservation and biodiversity

Animals shape ecosystems as predators, prey, pollinators, decomposers, and ecosystem engineers, and they drive biogeochemical processes in terrestrial and aquatic environments.

## What Makes an Organism an Animal

Zoologists identify animals by several defining traits:

- **Multicellularity** — many cells organized into tissues, with stable cell adhesion and coordinated differentiation
- **Eukaryotic cells** — nuclei, membrane-bound organelles, mitochondria, and a dynamic cytoskeleton
- **Ingestive heterotrophy** — food is ingested and digested internally; animals cannot photosynthesize
- **Specialized tissues** — muscle, nerve, epithelial, and connective tissues in most groups
- **Movement** — most animals move actively at some life stage; even sessile forms such as sponges typically have motile larvae
- **Sexual reproduction** — gamete-based reproduction is widespread, though many species also reproduce asexually
- **Embryonic development** — a blastula stage followed by gastrulation, which establishes germ layers and body axes

Metazoan multicellularity is further marked by collagen-based extracellular matrices, specialized cell–cell junctions, and conserved signaling pathways such as Wnt, Hedgehog, and Notch.

## How Animals Differ from Other Life Forms

- **Plants** — photosynthetic autotrophs with rigid cellulose cell walls
- **Fungi** — absorptive heterotrophs with chitin-based cell walls and no true embryonic germ layers
- **Protists** — a paraphyletic assemblage; some are multicellular but lack coordinated metazoan tissues and development
- **Bacteria and archaea** — unicellular prokaryotes that lack nuclei and metazoan developmental pathways

## The Scope of Animal Diversity

Animals range from extremely simple to highly complex and comprise more than 30 recognized phyla, each with a distinct body plan.

**Examples by complexity**

- Simple animals — sponges, jellyfish, corals, flatworms
- Intermediate complexity — insects, mollusks, crustaceans, amphibians
- Highly complex animals — birds, mammals, primates, humans

**Major evolutionary groups**

- **Basal metazoans** — Porifera, Placozoa, Cnidaria, Ctenophora
- **Protostomes** — Arthropoda, Mollusca, Annelida, Nematoda
- **Deuterostomes** — Chordata, Echinodermata, Hemichordata

Complexity ranges from diffuse nerve nets to highly centralized brains, and from hydrostatic skeletons to fully ossified endoskeletons. Major phyla include **Chordata**, **Arthropoda**, **Mollusca**, and **Cnidaria**.

## Evolutionary Origins

Animals likely evolved more than 600 million years ago in the Neoproterozoic from a colonial protist ancestor similar to modern choanoflagellates. Early animals diversified rapidly during the Cambrian explosion (~541 million years ago), giving rise to most major animal groups and innovations such as segmentation, mineralized skeletons, and complex sensory systems.

## Mathematical equation and formulas

Zoology itself is not built on a small closed set of “core equations,” but quantitative work on animals routinely uses a standard toolkit from ecology, physiology, allometry, and population genetics. Below are the most commonly applied formulas, with notation defined. These are the formulas most often attached to the topics in the earlier zoology outline (diversity, ecology, physiology, evolution of body plans). If you want a narrower set—only allometry, only population models, only phylogenetic comparative methods, or notation for a specific paper or textbook—specify the subfield.

### Population growth

**Exponential growth**

$$
\frac{dN}{dt} = rN
$$

$$
N(t) = N_0 e^{rt}
$$

- $N$: population size  
- $t$: time  
- $r$: intrinsic rate of increase  
- $N_0$: initial population size  

**Logistic growth**

$$
\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\left(1 - \frac{N}{K}\right)
$$

$$
N(t) = \frac{K}{1 + \left(\frac{K-N_0}{N_0}\right)e^{-rt}}
$$

- $K$: carrying capacity  

### Predator–prey (Lotka–Volterra)

$$
\frac{dN}{dt} = rN - aNP
$$

$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = eaNP - mP
$$

- $N$: prey density  
- $P$: predator density  
- $a$: attack rate  
- $e$: conversion efficiency  
- $m$: predator mortality rate  

### Allometric scaling

A trait $Y$ scales with body mass $M$ as

$$
Y = a M^{b}
$$

or in log form

$$
\log Y = \log a + b\log M
$$

Common exponents:
- Metabolic rate (Kleiber): $b \approx 0.75$
- Heart rate: $b \approx -0.25$
- Lifespan: $b \approx 0.25$

**Metabolic rate**

$$
B = B_0 M^{3/4}
$$

- $B$: basal or field metabolic rate  
- $B_0$: normalization constant  
- $M$: body mass  

### Species diversity

**Shannon index**

$$
H' = -\sum_{i=1}^{S} p_i \ln p_i
$$

- $S$: number of species  
- $p_i = n_i/N$: relative abundance of species $i$  
- $n_i$: individuals of species $i$  
- $N$: total individuals  

**Simpson index**

$$
D = \sum_{i=1}^{S} p_i^2
$$

$$
1-D \quad \text{(Gini–Simpson / probability of interspecific encounter)}
$$

**Species–area relationship**

$$
S = cA^{z}
$$

- $S$: number of species  
- $A$: area  
- $c, z$: fitted constants ($z$ often $\approx 0.25$)  

### Capture–recapture (Lincoln–Petersen)

$$
\hat{N} = \frac{n_1 n_2}{m}
$$

- $n_1$: marked in first sample  
- $n_2$: size of second sample  
- $m$: marked individuals recaptured  

### Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (population genetics)

For two alleles $A$ and $a$ with frequencies $p$ and $q$:

$$
p + q = 1
$$

$$
p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1
$$

- $p^2$: frequency of $AA$  
- $2pq$: frequency of $Aa$  
- $q^2$: frequency of $aa$  

### Body-size and geometric relationships

Surface area of a roughly isometric animal:

$$
A \propto L^{2} \propto M^{2/3}
$$

Volume / mass:

$$
V \propto L^{3} \propto M
$$

where $L$ is a linear dimension.

### Trophic transfer

Lindeman efficiency (approximate energy transfer between trophic levels):

$$
E = \frac{P_{n+1}}{P_n} \approx 0.10
$$

- $P_n$: production at trophic level $n$
