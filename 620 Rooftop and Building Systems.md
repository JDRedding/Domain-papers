While most discussion of rooftop and building-integrated systems focuses on photovoltaics, a wide range of non-photovoltaic technologies also contributes to structural integrity, energy efficiency, and sustainability. Understanding these systems is essential for architects, engineers, and developers designing modern buildings.

Excluding photovoltaics, rooftop and building-integrated systems include green roofs, cool roofs, thermal insulation, daylighting, water management, fire protection, and integrated envelope components. Together, these systems improve energy efficiency, occupant comfort, and building resilience. They often serve multiple functions while reducing costs and environmental impact. Strategic selection and early-stage integration help modern buildings meet both performance requirements and sustainability goals.

## 1. Rooftop Systems (Non-PV)

### 1.1 Green Roofs

**Definition:** Vegetative layers installed on roofs that provide insulation, stormwater management, and aesthetic benefits.

**Components:** Waterproof membrane, root barrier, drainage layer, growing medium, and vegetation.

**Benefits:**
- Thermal insulation that can reduce heating and cooling loads by roughly 15–30%
- Extended roof-membrane life by shielding the surface from UV exposure
- Mitigation of the urban heat-island effect

**Considerations:** Requires structural load analysis and ongoing maintenance for irrigation and plant health.

### 1.2 Cool Roofs

**Definition:** Roof surfaces designed to reflect more sunlight and absorb less heat.

**Materials:** Reflective coatings, tiles, and membranes with a high solar reflectance index (SRI).

**Benefits:**
- Can reduce roof surface temperature by 30–50°F
- Lowers building cooling costs, especially in hot climates
- Can be applied to existing roof materials such as asphalt, metal, and concrete

**Limitations:** Less effective under continuous shading; reflectivity can degrade without maintenance.

### 1.3 Roof Insulation and Thermal Barriers

**Definition:** Layers added to roof assemblies to improve a building’s thermal performance.

**Types:** Rigid boards (polyisocyanurate, extruded polystyrene), spray foam, and blown-in insulation.

**Integration:** Applied above or within roof decks and compatible with both sloped and flat roofs.

**Benefits:**
- Reduces thermal bridging
- Helps meet energy codes such as IECC and ASHRAE 90.1
- Improves occupant comfort

### 1.4 Stormwater Management Systems

**Definition:** Rooftop systems for water capture, storage, and controlled discharge.

**Examples:** Rainwater harvesting tanks, detention and storage layers in green roofs, and permeable pavers on roof decks.

**Benefits:**
- Reduces municipal stormwater runoff
- Enables reuse for irrigation or other non-potable purposes

**Design considerations:** Structural load, waterproofing, and overflow provisions.

### 1.5 Rooftop Safety and Access Systems

**Examples:** Guardrails, catwalks, anchor points, skylight protection, and photovoltaic-ready mounts (without PV installed).

**Purpose:** Code compliance, maintenance access, and fall protection.

**Standards:** OSHA; IBC Sections 1501–1511.

## 2. Building-Integrated Systems (Non-PV)

### 2.1 Building Envelope Integration

**Definition:** Systems embedded in the façade, roof, or windows to provide structural, thermal, or functional enhancement.

**Examples:**
- **Insulated wall panels (IWP):** Structural support, thermal insulation, and moisture control
- **Architectural cladding systems:** Metal panels, fiber cement, terracotta, or stone veneer used in place of conventional façades
- **Curtain walls:** High-performance glazing integrated with insulation and shading devices

### 2.2 Daylighting and Shading Systems

**Definition:** Elements designed to optimize natural light while controlling heat gain.

**Examples:**
- Integrated louvers and shading screens mounted on or built into the façade
- Skylights and light tubes that daylight interior spaces
- Electrochromic or smart glass that modulates light transmission to balance energy performance and occupant comfort

### 2.3 Energy and Environmental Control Systems

**Rooftop mechanical integration:**
- HVAC equipment placement with vibration isolation and thermal buffers
- Rain screens and ventilated façades that improve heat dissipation
- Integration with building automation systems (BAS/BMS) to control temperature, humidity, and airflow

### 2.4 Water and Waste Systems

- **Blue roofs:** Temporarily store rainwater to mitigate peak runoff
- **Rooftop greywater systems:** Store and reuse non-potable water

### 2.5 Fire Safety and Code-Compliant Features

**Integrated fire barriers:** Parapets with non-combustible caps, fire-rated roof assemblies, and penetration flashings.

**Compliance:** ICC IBC Chapter 15 (Roof Assemblies and Rooftop Structures).

## 3. Materials and Structural Considerations

**Roofing materials:** Asphalt shingles, clay and concrete tiles, metal panels, and modified bitumen membranes.

**Load considerations:** Snow, water, green-roof planting media, mechanical equipment, and maintenance loads.

**Durability and lifespan:** Specify UV- and weather-resistant membranes. Typical service life ranges from 20 to 50 years.

## 4. Design Best Practices

- **Early integration:** Coordinate rooftop and envelope systems during design to optimize load, aesthetics, and energy performance.
- **Multi-functionality:** Combine structural, thermal, rainfall-management, and daylighting functions to reduce material redundancy.
- **Maintenance planning:** Include access paths, inspection points, and fallback drainage for long-term reliability.
- **Code compliance:** Follow IBC 2024 Sections 1501–1511 for rooftop construction, flashings, parapets, and wind- and impact-resistant materials.
- **Synergy with renewable systems (optional):** Design for future solar or HVAC integration without compromising structural integrity.

### 1. Roof surface energy balance

Net heat flux into the roof surface:

$$
q_\mathrm{net} = \alpha I_s - \varepsilon\sigma\!\left(T_s^4-T_\mathrm{sky}^4\right) - h_c\!\left(T_s-T_a\right) - q_\mathrm{cond}
$$

Absorbed solar:

$$
q_\mathrm{solar}=\alpha I_s=(1-\rho)\,I_s
$$

Emitted longwave:

$$
q_\mathrm{lw}=\varepsilon\sigma T_s^4
$$

Net longwave to sky:

$$
q_\mathrm{lw,net}=\varepsilon\sigma\!\left(T_s^4-T_\mathrm{sky}^4\right)
$$

Convection to outdoor air:

$$
q_\mathrm{conv}=h_c\!\left(T_s-T_a\right)
$$

Conduction into the assembly:

$$
q_\mathrm{cond}=U\!\left(T_s-T_\mathrm{in}\right)
$$

Steady state ($q_\mathrm{net}=0$) for a well-insulated cool roof often reduces to:

$$
\alpha I_s = \varepsilon\sigma\!\left(T_s^4-T_\mathrm{sky}^4\right) + h_c\!\left(T_s-T_a\right)
$$

### 2. Cool roofs: reflectance, emittance, SRI

Solar absorptance:

$$
\alpha=1-\rho
$$

A practical estimate of surface-temperature reduction versus a dark roof:

$$
\Delta T_s \approx \frac{(\alpha_\mathrm{dark}-\alpha_\mathrm{cool})I_s}{h_c+4\varepsilon\sigma T_a^3}
$$

ASTM E1980 Solar Reflectance Index compares a surface with reference black ($\rho=0.05$, $\varepsilon=0.90$) and white ($\rho=0.80$, $\varepsilon=0.90$) surfaces under standard conditions:

$$
\mathrm{SRI}=100\,\frac{T_b-T_s}{T_b-T_w}
$$

where $T_s$, $T_b$, and $T_w$ are the steady-state temperatures of the specimen, reference black, and reference white.

Equivalent quadratic fit used in practice:

$$
\chi=\frac{\alpha}{\varepsilon^{0.75}\,h_c^{0.25}}, \qquad \mathrm{SRI}=123.97-141.35\chi+9.655\chi^2
$$

($\chi$ evaluated with the ASTM standard convection coefficients: low / medium / high wind.)

### 3. Insulation, $R$-value, and $U$-factor

Layer resistance:

$$
R_i=\frac{L_i}{k_i}
$$

Air-film resistances $R_o$ (outside) and $R_i$ (inside) are added to material layers.

Total resistance:

$$
R_\mathrm{tot} = R_o+\sum_i R_i+R_\mathrm{in}
$$

Assembly $U$-factor:

$$
U=\frac{1}{R_\mathrm{tot}}
$$

Heat flux through the roof:

$$
q=U\!\left(T_\mathrm{in}-T_\mathrm{out}\right)
$$

or, using sol-air temperature $T_\mathrm{sol-air}$:

$$
q=U\!\left(T_\mathrm{in}-T_\mathrm{sol-air}\right)
$$

$$
T_\mathrm{sol-air} = T_a + \frac{\alpha I_s}{h_c} - \frac{\varepsilon\Delta R}{h_c}
$$

where $\Delta R$ is the longwave correction to the sky.

Parallel-path / thermal-bridging estimate:

$$
U_\mathrm{eff} = a_\mathrm{clear}\,U_\mathrm{clear} + a_\mathrm{bridge}\,U_\mathrm{bridge}
$$

with area fractions $a_\mathrm{clear}+a_\mathrm{bridge}=1$.

Area-weighted mean $R$:

$$
R_\mathrm{avg} = \left(\sum_j \frac{a_j}{R_j}\right)^{-1}
$$

Approximate heating/cooling load change from added insulation:

$$
\Delta Q = A\,(U_\mathrm{old}-U_\mathrm{new})\,\mathrm{HDD}\quad\text{or}\quad\mathrm{CDD}
$$

(times 24 h if HDD/CDD are in K·day).

---

### 4. Green roofs: heat and mass

Effective conduction through saturated/unsaturated media:

$$
q_\mathrm{gr} = \frac{T_\mathrm{canopy}-T_\mathrm{membrane}}{R_\mathrm{media}+R_\mathrm{drain}}
$$

Latent heat from evapotranspiration:

$$
q_\mathrm{ET}=\lambda\,E
$$

where $E$ is evapotranspiration mass flux and $\lambda\approx 2.45\times10^6\,\mathrm{J/kg}$.

A Penman–Monteith form for $E$:

$$
E = \frac{\Delta(R_n-G)+\rho_a c_p(e_s-e_a)/r_a}{\lambda\!\left[\Delta+\gamma(1+r_s/r_a)\right]}
$$

Saturated (wet) green-roof load:

$$
w_\mathrm{wet} = \gamma_\mathrm{dry}D_\mathrm{media} + \gamma_w\theta_\mathrm{sat}D_\mathrm{media} + w_\mathrm{plants} + w_\mathrm{buildup}
$$

Water stored in media:

$$
V_\mathrm{ret} = A\,n\,D_\mathrm{media}\,S_r
$$

where $n$ is porosity and $S_r$ is retained saturation.

---

### 5. Stormwater, blue roofs, harvest

Rational-method peak runoff:

$$
Q_p=C\,i\,A
$$

with rainfall intensity $i$ for the design storm.

Continuity for a roof cistern / blue roof:

$$
\frac{dV}{dt}=Q_\mathrm{in}-Q_\mathrm{out}-Q_\mathrm{overflow}
$$

$$
Q_\mathrm{in}=P\,A_\mathrm{catch}
$$

Controlled blue-roof release (orifice approximation):

$$
Q_\mathrm{out}=C_d A_o\sqrt{2gH}
$$

Required detention volume for a design event (simplified):

$$
V_\mathrm{req} = \int_0^{t_d}\!\big(Q_\mathrm{in}(t)-Q_\mathrm{allow}\big)\,dt
$$

Harvest yield:

$$
V_\mathrm{harvest}=\eta\,\rho_c\,P\,A
$$

where $\eta$ is collection efficiency and $\rho_c$ is a dimensionless capture factor ($\rho_c\le 1$).

### 6. Daylighting and glazing

Transmitted solar heat through glazing:

$$
q_\mathrm{sol}=\mathrm{SHGC}\,I_s
$$

Visible light flux:

$$
E_v=\mathrm{VT}\,E_{v,\mathrm{ext}}
$$

Daylight factor (overcast-sky definition):

$$
\mathrm{DF} = \frac{E_{v,\mathrm{int}}}{E_{v,\mathrm{ext}}}\times 100\%
$$

Simple shading of direct beam:

$$
I_\mathrm{shaded}=I_b\cos\theta\cdot(1-f_\mathrm{shade})+I_d
$$

where $f_\mathrm{shade}$ is the shaded fraction of the aperture, $I_b$ beam, $I_d$ diffuse.

Smart / electrochromic glass:

$$
\mathrm{SHGC}=\mathrm{SHGC}(V),\qquad \mathrm{VT}=\mathrm{VT}(V)
$$

with $V$ the control state (clear $\to$ tinted).

---

### 7. Structural loads

Generic distributed roof load:

$$
w_\mathrm{total} = w_\mathrm{DL}+w_\mathrm{LL}+w_\mathrm{SL}+w_\mathrm{RL}+w_\mathrm{equip}
$$

Snow (model form; use the governing code for coefficients):

$$
w_\mathrm{SL}=C_e C_t I_s p_g
$$

Wind uplift on roof covering (model form):

$$
p=q_h\,(GC_p-GC_{pi})
$$

Green-roof / blue-roof rain load from ponded depth $D$:

$$
w_\mathrm{water}=\gamma_w D
$$

Example strength combination (form only; apply the code in force):

$$
1.2\,\mathrm{DL}+1.6\,\mathrm{SL}+0.5\,\mathrm{LL}
$$

$$
1.2\,\mathrm{DL}+1.0\,\mathrm{WL}+0.5\,\mathrm{SL}
$$

Deflection check:

$$
\delta\le\frac{\ell}{n}
$$

with $n$ from the serviceability limit (e.g. 180, 240).

---

### 8. Compact design set

Cool-roof target:

$$
\rho\uparrow,\ \varepsilon\uparrow\ \Rightarrow\ T_s\downarrow\ \Rightarrow\ q_\mathrm{cool}\downarrow
$$

Envelope heat flow:

$$
q=U(T_\mathrm{in}-T_\mathrm{sol-air})
$$

Stormwater control:

$$
Q_p=Cia,\qquad V=\int(Q_\mathrm{in}-Q_\mathrm{out})\,dt
$$

Green-roof wet load:

$$
w_\mathrm{wet}=\gamma_\mathrm{sat}D+w_\mathrm{plants}+w_\mathrm{system}
$$

Glazing solar gain:

$$
q_\mathrm{sol}=\mathrm{SHGC}\,I_s
$$
