**Education** is both what is taught and what is learned. Around the world it takes many institutional forms, from state classrooms and private academies to home instruction, trade training, and universities.

> The act or process of teaching and learning; the knowledge, skill, or judgment gained through that process; a course or level of instruction; the field that studies teaching and learning; or, more loosely, any experience that instructs or enlightens.

People speak of *getting an education*, *working in education*, *investing in education*, or *paying for someone’s education*. The word can mean the process (“education begins at home”), the content (“a broad education”), the institution or profession (“she works in education”), or a subject of study (“a course in education”).

## Forms of Learning

Education covers many arrangements, among them elementary, secondary, and higher education; coeducation and special education; vocational and continuing education; physical education; coursework, examinations, and work-study programs. Systems differ by country in names for degrees, school stages, exams, and offices, but the underlying idea remains organized teaching and learning.

## Core Meanings

1. **The process.** Acquiring or imparting knowledge, especially in a systematic way through childhood, adolescence, and later formal study.
2. **The result.** What a person knows or can do after instruction, training, or study.
3. **A program or level.** A specified kind or stage of instruction, such as driver education, a college education, or professional training.
4. **The academic field.** The theory and practice of teaching and learning; pedagogy.
5. **An enlightening experience.** Informal usage: “Working there was an education.”

Related senses treat education as upbringing and social training—the habits, judgment, and manners formed over time—not only classroom knowledge.

Education may refer to:

- the **activity** of instructing
- the **profession** of teaching
- the **gradual process** of acquiring knowledge
- a **body of knowledge** already gained
- in U.S. government usage, the **Department of Education**

## Education Systems

Education systems worldwide differ in structure, governance, and method. They include public, private, homeschool, vocational, alternative, international, and higher-education arrangements.

### Public (state) education

Public systems are funded and run by governments and offer free or subsidized schooling. Curricula are typically standardized to support wide access. Examples include conventional elementary, middle, and high schools in the United States and comparable state systems elsewhere.

### Private education

Private schools are funded mainly through tuition, donations, or endowments. They may offer specialized curricula, smaller classes, or religious instruction. The category includes independent K–12 schools, boarding schools, academies, and, for example, many Catholic or preparatory schools.

### Charter and magnet schools

**Charter schools** are publicly funded but operate with more independence from some local rules and often emphasize particular methods or programs.

**Magnet schools** concentrate on fields such as STEM, the arts, or international studies and usually draw students by interest rather than by neighborhood alone.

### Homeschooling

Parents or guardians educate children at home, from early years through high school. Curricula and pacing can be tailored to the student. Legal requirements vary by country and, in the United States, by state.

### Vocational and technical education

Vocational education trains students for specific kinds of work—trades, healthcare, information technology, and related fields. It often appears in high school or post-secondary programs and is meant to prepare students for skilled employment.

### International and alternative systems

**International schools** may use curricula such as the International Baccalaureate or British A-Levels and often serve expatriate families or students seeking globally recognized qualifications.

**Alternative approaches**, including Montessori, Waldorf, and Reggio Emilia, stress self-directed learning, creativity, and broader child development rather than a single standard classroom model.

### Higher education

Universities, colleges, and professional schools offer undergraduate, graduate, and vocational credentials beyond K–12. Degree structures, requirements, and teaching methods vary widely by country.

## How Systems Are Classified

Education systems can be grouped by funding, governance, focus, and method:

- Public or state-funded schools
- Private and independent schools
- Charter and magnet schools
- Homeschooling
- Vocational and technical institutions
- International and alternative programs
- Higher-education institutions

Each arrangement serves different needs, cultures, and aims.

## Older Images and Comparisons

Writers have long described education through metaphor: a seed that grows; writing on stone in youth and on sand in age; rowing upstream, so that standing still is to drift back; a candle that lights others while it burns. Such images treat education as formation over time, not a single event.

## Related Terms

Common near-synonyms, depending on context, include *instruction*, *teaching*, *schooling*, *training*, *tuition*, *pedagogy*, *learning*, *scholarship*, and *edification*.

### In Other Languages

In Spanish, common equivalents include *educación*, *enseñanza*, *formación*, and *estudios*, with *pedagogía* used for the academic subject. Exact choice depends on whether the sense is upbringing, schooling, training, or culture.
