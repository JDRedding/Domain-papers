**Commerce, communications, and transportation** are closely linked systems. Commerce is the exchange of goods, services, and ideas. Transportation moves people and freight so that exchange can take place. Communications carries the information that makes trade, travel, and organization possible.

Transportation moves what commerce exchanges. Communications coordinates both: schedules, prices, contracts, navigation, safety warnings, and public information. Together they form much of the infrastructure of modern economic and social life.

- Commerce creates the demand to move goods and people.
- Transportation supplies the physical means of movement.
- Communications supplies the information that directs movement and exchange.

**Commerce** is large-scale exchange. **Transportation** is the organized movement of people and goods. **Communications** is the transmission of meaning through language, gesture, media, and networks. Each can be studied on its own; in practice they operate as one system of trade, mobility, and information.

## Commerce

**Commerce** is the buying and selling of goods and services, especially on a large scale between cities, regions, or nations. Related senses include social or intellectual exchange.

Commerce includes trade, marketing, import and export, payment, and distribution. It may be domestic or international, face-to-face or electronic. **E-commerce** is trade conducted through digital networks.

In government, *Commerce* may also name a department that promotes trade and related economic activity. Near-synonyms include *business*, *trade*, *industry*, and *traffic* (in the older sense of commercial dealing). In Spanish, a common equivalent is *comercio*.

Transport is part of commerce: goods have little market value if they cannot reach buyers. Communications is part of commerce as well: prices, contracts, advertising, and logistics all depend on the flow of information.

## Transportation

**Transportation** is the movement of people, animals, and goods from one place to another. It depends on infrastructure, vehicles, and organized operations, and it underpins daily life, trade, and economic growth.

A working system usually combines:

- **Infrastructure** — roads, railways, airports, seaports, pipelines, and related facilities
- **Vehicles** — cars, buses, trucks, trains, bicycles, aircraft, and ships
- **Operations** — scheduling, management, safety, and logistics

### Modes of transport

**Land.** Road and rail are the main forms. Rail includes freight trains, intercity passenger service, trams or streetcars, commuter rail, and high-speed systems. Road transport offers flexible, often door-to-door movement by car, bus, and truck, but it requires large amounts of space and ongoing maintenance.

**Air.** Aircraft, helicopters, and drones support fast long-distance travel. Airports handle passengers and cargo.

**Water.** Ships, barges, and boats move people and goods on rivers, lakes, and oceans. Harbors and canals support loading, unloading, and related work.

**Other modes.** Walking, cycling, cable systems, pipelines, and, in some places, animal-powered transport remain important. Human-powered travel can support shorter trips and more sustainable local movement.

### Public and private systems

Transportation may be public, private, or mixed. Public transit typically includes buses, rail, ferries, and paratransit. Private options include personal vehicles, commercial trucking, taxis, and ride-share services. Airports, ports, and highway networks usually serve both passenger travel and freight.

Planning agencies at local, regional, and national levels maintain roads and bridges, set transit policy, and try to balance safety, cost, access, and environmental effects.

## Communications

**Communications** is the process of sending information, messages, or meaning from a sender to a receiver through a channel. It is central to personal life, organizations, and technical systems.

### Definition and scope

Communication may produce shared understanding or influence. It includes:

- **Verbal** forms — speech, writing, and sign language
- **Nonverbal** forms — gesture, facial expression, posture, and tone

Researchers also study signaling in animals and plants.

### Types

- **Interpersonal.** Exchange between people, in person or through technology; listening and feedback matter.
- **Intrapersonal.** Communication within the self, such as reflection or problem-solving.
- **Verbal.** Language in spoken, written, or signed form.
- **Nonverbal.** Cues that carry meaning without words.
- **Mass communication.** Messages to large audiences through radio, television, print, and the internet.
- **Organizational and public communication.** Information managed by institutions, including governments, companies, and official announcements.

### Models

Common models describe a sender, message, channel, receiver, and feedback:

- **Linear models** (for example, Shannon–Weaver and Lasswell) treat communication as one-way transmission.
- **Interaction models** (for example, Schramm) add feedback between sender and receiver.
- **Transactional models** (for example, Barnlund) treat sending and receiving as simultaneous and stress shared meaning.

### Technology and institutions

Modern communications include telecommunication systems, internet networks, and computer-mediated exchange. Public agencies use websites, broadcasts, alerts, and social media to reach residents. Organizations use communications offices to keep internal and external messages clear.

Effective communication needs a clear message, a workable channel, and a way to receive feedback. Context matters in personal, mass, and institutional settings.
