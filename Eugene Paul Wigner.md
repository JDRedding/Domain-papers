# **Eugene Paul Wigner****
Eugene Paul Wigner (1902–1995)** was a Hungarian‑American theoretical physicist whose introduction of **group theory** into quantum mechanics reshaped modern physics. He received the **1963 Nobel Prize in Physics** for applying symmetry principles to nuclear and particle physics.  Wigner’s students included **John Bardeen**, **Victor Weisskopf**, **Frederick Seitz**, and many others. His collected works span **500+ papers**, and his concepts permeate modern physics. He died **January 1, 1995**, in Princeton.  He is also widely remembered for his influential essay **“The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences” (1960)**

### ⚛️ **Manhattan Project & Nuclear Engineering**
- In **1939**, Wigner and **Leó Szilárd** helped draft the famous **Einstein–Szilárd letter** urging Roosevelt to consider nuclear chain reactions. 
- At the **Metallurgical Laboratory (Chicago)**, he led theoretical reactor design, contributing to the **water‑cooled plutonium production reactors** at Hanford.  
- Present at the first controlled nuclear chain reaction (**Chicago Pile‑1**, December 2, 1942).

### 🧠 **Major Scientific Contributions**
While in Berlin, he attended lectures by **Einstein, Planck, and Nernst**, which catalyzed his shift toward theoretical physics (supported by biographical sources, though often noted in memoirs rather than formal citations). Wigner’s work established symmetry as a foundational organizing principle in physics:

- **Wigner’s theorem** — symmetries correspond to **unitary or anti‑unitary operators**.  
- **Wigner D‑matrices** and the **Wigner–Eckart theorem** — essential for angular momentum theory.  
- **Poincaré group representations** — foundational for classifying particles by **mass and spin**.  
- **Nuclear physics**: parity, short‑range nuclear forces, neutron absorption theory (1936).  
- Concepts named after him include the **Wigner distribution**, **Wigner crystal**, **Wigner–Seitz cell**, **Wigner energy**, and many others. 

These contributions remain central in **quantum information**, **condensed matter**, **nuclear engineering**, and **mathematical physics**.

This is exactly why some authors casually call it the **Wigner point**.
